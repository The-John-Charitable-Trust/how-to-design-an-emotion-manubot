# Chapter 6: The Blueprint of Mind

*The Theory of Constrained Identity*

*In chapter 5, I ended the false dichotomy between structure and variation by assigning classical and prototype theories different explanatory responsibilities. Classical theory specifies identity conditions. Prototype theory explains categorization over admissible instances.*

*Rather than treating prototype theory as an account of conceptual identity, I recast it as a theory of categorization operating downstream from conceptual identity. Chapter 6 builds the architecture that makes this division of explanatory labor possible.*

*A concept cannot survive when theorists relocate its identity conditions downstream into similarity, use, prediction, habit, culture, or performance. Those phenomena matter. They explain how rational agents and computational systems instantiate concepts, categorize admissible instances, evaluate performance, learn from experience, and optimize execution. Each presupposes the concept’s intensional structure rather than accounting for it.*

*This chapter gives the concept its computational architecture.*

*I propose the Goal-Governed Model of Conceptual Identity. The model separates four functions that theory too often conflates: conceptual identity, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation.*

*Once I distinguish these functions, I no longer have to choose between structure and variability because each occupies a different level of the architecture.*

*The constraint layer specifies identity conditions and admissibility. The instantiation layer realizes those conditions under determinate values. The categorization layer organizes admissible instances according to similarity, typicality, contextual relevance, and graded membership without redefining conceptual identity. The goal layer regulates those categorized instances relative to governing purposes.*

*This is the key: identity conditions do not perform every explanatory task. They do not explain similarity, typicality, usefulness, priority, error correction, learning, or optimization by themselves. They define the admissible space within which those downstream processes operate.*

*Similarity belongs downstream. Categorization belongs downstream. Learning belongs downstream. Optimization belongs downstream. Prototype effects belong downstream. Similarity, categorization, prototype effects, learning, and optimization all contribute to computational execution, by which I mean the operational application of conceptual identity to admissible instances. None of these downstream processes, however, generates the identity conditions they presuppose.*

*The model therefore rests on five computational invariants. Identity conditions remain stable unless the constraint structure changes. Every admissible instance satisfies those identity conditions before categorization begins. Categorization organizes admissible instances without redefining conceptual identity. Goals regulate evaluation rather than admissibility. Learning and optimization refine computational execution without altering the identity conditions they presuppose. Consequently, no downstream success condition alone constitutes a concept.*

*The result is a computational architecture that preserves stable identity conditions while permitting flexible execution. Those identity conditions authorize meaningful variation, and downstream computational processes realize rather than replace conceptual identity.*

*This chapter completes the architecture of conceptual identity. But architecture alone does not yet explain meaning. Identity conditions determine what may count as an admissible instance. They do not yet explain how concepts come to stand for anything beyond themselves.*

*That is the task of Chapter 7, The Constructivist Semiotics: The Architecture of Meaning. There I explain how constraint structures acquire semantic significance, how constructs come to stand for their referents, and how interpretation binds conceptual identity to meaning.*

CHAPTER VII

The Constructivist Semiotics

The Architecture of Meaning

In Chapter 5, I resolved the classical-prototype dichotomy by placing structure and variation at different levels of explanation. I do not need to choose between them. I need an architecture that accommodates both.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-1">1</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-2">2</a></sup>

That is the task of this chapter.

I now propose the Goal-Governed Model of Conceptual Identity. The model fixes identity conditions upstream and preserves variability downstream through instantiation, categorization, goal-directed evaluation, learning, and computational execution.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-3">3</a></sup> It explains how a concept can remain stable without becoming rigid, and how instances can vary without erasing the concept they instantiate.

I separate four architectural functions so that each performs a distinct explanatory role: conceptual identity, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation. Computational execution operates over those functions through similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization. A concept supplies identity conditions. Instantiation realizes those conditions under determinate values. Prototype-guided categorization organizes admissible instances according to similarity, typicality, contextual relevance, and graded membership. Goal-directed evaluation regulates those categorized instances. When theory collapses these functions, it mistakes variation for identity, performance for meaning, and categorization for definition.

This architecture also clarifies the proper role of prototype theory. In the previous chapter, I argued that prototype theory explains categorization over conceptually admissible instances rather than conceptual identity. That computational refactoring now follows directly from the architecture. Constraint structures specify identity conditions. Instantiation realizes admissible instances. Prototype effects operate over those admissible instances by explaining similarity, typicality, contextual salience, and graded membership. They do not determine what counts as an admissible instance in the first place. Goal-directed evaluation then regulates those categorized instances. Prototype theory therefore occupies a downstream explanatory role within the Goal-Governed Model rather than competing with the constraint layer for conceptual identity.

In this chapter, I therefore move from critique to specification. I no longer ask why existing theories fail to preserve identity conditions. I now state the architecture that preserves them.

I call this model computational because it distinguishes architectural functions from the computational processes that operate over them. In Chapter 4, I argued that many contemporary theories orphan identity conditions by relocating them downstream into categorization, prediction, learning, and performance. In Appendix C, Reconstructing Goal-Based Concepts, I showed that goals regulate the application of concepts without generating the identity conditions that make those concepts possible. The Goal-Governed Model of Conceptual Identity generalizes that reconstruction into a layered computational architecture. Identity conditions specify admissibility. Instantiation realizes admissible instances. Categorization organizes those instances. Goal-directed evaluation regulates them. Computational execution then applies similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization without redefining conceptual identity.

This model is computational because it distinguishes architectural structures from the computational processes that operate over them. Constraint, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation define the architecture. Similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization execute that architecture without constituting it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-5">5</a></sup>

I begin with the first and governing layer: the constraint layer. Before an agent uses a concept, learns it, categorizes by it, or evaluates through it, the concept must have conditions of identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-6">6</a></sup>

A concept is an intensional constraint structure. It specifies the internal rule structure that determines what may count as a valid instance. It is not a list of observed cases. It is not a region of similarity. It is not a statistical summary. It is the set of structural requirements that governs permissible combinations of properties and relations.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-7">7</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-8">8</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-9">9</a></sup>

Identity conditions fix conceptual sameness. They determine what makes a concept the same concept across time, agents, and contexts. They do not specify every particular value an instance may take. Instead, they specify the permissible relations within which those values may count as instances at all.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-10">10</a></sup>

Admissibility follows from identity conditions. A candidate instance counts as admissible only if it satisfies the constraints the concept specifies. Before an agent sorts, ranks, evaluates, or uses an instance, the instance must first belong to the admissible space defined by those identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-11">11</a></sup>

An instance is a determinate realization of a concept within that admissible space. It assigns values, resolves variables, and places the concept under concrete conditions. Variation among instances reflects execution rather than identity. Differences in magnitude, configuration, embodiment, context, or implementation therefore represent different realizations of the same concept rather than different concepts. Environmental input, bodily state, and physiological process may shape execution, but they do not supply conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-12">12</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-13">13</a></sup>

This distinction explains why concepts are non-extensional. A concept is not identical to the set of its instances. Instances may multiply, disappear, cluster, scatter, or vary in distribution without changing the concept. Extension depends on what occurs across contexts. Intension governs what may count as an occurrence in the first place. The actual never exhausts the possible.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-14">14</a></sup>

The distinction between concepts and categories is equally important. A category groups admissible instances relative to purposes or norms. It may track typicality, frequency, usefulness, danger, elegance, efficiency, or practical relevance. But conceptual identity does not rise and fall with those groupings. A shift in category boundaries does not, by itself, revise the concept.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-15">15</a></sup>

A simple illustration clarifies the point. Suppose a concept requires a defined structural relation among elements. Different instances may realize that relation under different values, magnitudes, or contexts. As long as the governing relation remains intact, the identity conditions remain stable.

The constraint layer therefore fixes identity conditions, determines admissibility, distinguishes concepts from their instances and categories, and resists extensional reduction. It provides the stable architecture within which downstream instantiation, categorization, evaluation, learning, and optimization may occur.

Having established the constraint layer, I now turn to the first downstream layer: instantiation. The constraint layer fixes identity conditions; the instantiation layer realizes those conditions under determinate values. Instantiation gives admissible concepts concrete realization, but realization alone does not determine how those realizations will be interpreted, organized, or evaluated. To account for those downstream functions, I next introduce the goal layer. Goals specify the criteria by which agents later categorize and evaluate admissible instances. They do not specify identity conditions. They regulate the use of instances that already satisfy them.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-16">16</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-17">17</a></sup>

I locate variation at the level of goal selection, prioritization, and contextual application. Different domains, communities, institutions, or systems may apply different regulatory criteria to the same concept and its admissible instances. They may value different features, impose different thresholds, adopt different standards of performance, or use context to determine which constraint structure a lexical term invokes. Those differences generate different evaluations without multiplying concepts because they regulate the application of identity conditions rather than redefining them. Identity conditions remain stable unless agents revise the admissibility conditions themselves. When agents change goals, thresholds, priorities, or contextual selections, they change evaluation rather than conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-18">18</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-19">19</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-20">20</a></sup>

If a term like right indexes a moral claim in one context and a legal entitlement in another, the shift does not prove that the concept is fuzzy or constructed on the fly. It proves that the same linguistic pointer can access different stable architectures. Each architecture maintains its own identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-21">21</a></sup>

The rule is simple: the constraint structure fixes identity conditions. If the constraint changes, the concept changes. If the constraint remains and only the realization varies, identity remains intact. What appears to be conceptual change often reflects modifications to instantiation, categorization, or goal evaluation rather than to the identity conditions themselves.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-22">22</a></sup>

A goal is a regulatory criterion applied to admissible instances. It specifies standards of adequacy, efficiency, relevance, or compliance relative to a particular purpose. Goals regulate admissible instances after they have satisfied identity conditions; they do not determine what may count as an instance in the first place.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-23">23</a></sup>

The architecture now contains the elements required for categorization and evaluation. Concepts fix admissibility. Instantiation realizes admissible instances. Goals specify the criteria by which agents later categorize and evaluate those instances. Identity conditions determine what may count as an instance. Goal-directed evaluation determines how well categorized instances satisfy those criteria.

I can now assign categorization its proper explanatory role. Categorization groups admissible instances according to governing goals. It operates over determinate realizations that already satisfy conceptual constraints. It does not fix conceptual identity. It does not determine admissibility.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-24">24</a></sup>

We compute similarity relative to governing goals. No instance is similar in the abstract. We compare admissible instances by selecting dimensions, thresholds, and priorities that reflect those goals. The same admissible instances can support different categorizations under different goals without changing the concept.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-25">25</a></sup>

I evaluate categorized instances relative to governing criteria. Success and failure indicate how well an admissible instance satisfies the selected criterion. An instance may satisfy the concept and still fail the governing goal. Identity determines what something is; evaluation determines how well it serves a particular purpose.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-26">26</a></sup>

I can distinguish correct from incorrect categorization only because identity conditions remain stable. A misclassification is possible only when fixed identity conditions exist against which I can compare an instance. If conceptual identity depended on successful categorization, no principled distinction between correct and incorrect grouping would remain. Disagreement would become nothing more than divergent clustering habits.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-27">27</a></sup>

I locate correction at the level of goal-directed evaluation. When categorized instances fail to satisfy governing criteria, agents adjust execution strategies, similarity metrics, thresholds, priorities, or goal weightings rather than revising conceptual identity. Conceptual identity remains stable while its application improves.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-28">28</a></sup>

Up to this point, I have specified the architecture of conceptual identity. I now distinguish that architecture from the computational processes that operate over it. Constraint, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation define the architecture. Similarity computation, evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization execute that architecture without constituting it.

Learning refines how agents apply conceptual constraints. Agents adjust parameters, stabilize interpretive habits, and improve how they generate, select, group, and weight admissible instances relative to governing goals. They do so by responding to prediction errors, feedback, and other discrepancies between expected and observed outcomes. Those adjustments refine execution without revising conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-29">29</a></sup>

Optimization likewise operates within a fixed constraint structure. Systems may update parameters, recalibrate thresholds, or refine execution strategies, but those adjustments presuppose a stable conceptual architecture. Engineers define loss functions to represent goal criteria formally. Minimizing those functions improves performance relative to specified goals without determining what the system is about. The identity of the task, the admissible mappings, and the representational role of the output must already be specified.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-30">30</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-31">31</a></sup>

No computational process generates identity conditions. Learning, optimization, evaluation, and correction all presuppose the constraint structure within which they operate. When agents respond to prediction errors by revising parameters, thresholds, or execution strategies, conceptual identity remains intact while performance improves. Only when agents revise the identity conditions themselves do they revise the concept. That distinction separates improved execution from conceptual revision.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-32">32</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-33">33</a></sup>

This distinction also makes the Goal-Governed Model compatible with computational implementation without reducing conceptual identity to statistical convergence. Learning refines execution. Optimization improves performance relative to governing goals. Neither generates the identity conditions that make those processes intelligible.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-34">34</a></sup>

The architecture therefore rests on four structural invariants.

Identity. Conceptual identity resides at the constraint layer as an intensional structure that fixes admissibility conditions. Every downstream process presupposes those identity conditions rather than generating them.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-35">35</a></sup>

Instantiation. Execution occurs through admissible realizations of those identity conditions. Instantiation, embodiment, contextual realization, and implementation produce variation without revising conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-36">36</a></sup>

Categorization and Goal-Directed Evaluation. Categorization organizes admissible instances according to similarity, typicality, contextual relevance, and graded membership. Goal-directed evaluation then regulates those categorized instances according to governing criteria. Neither function determines conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-37">37</a></sup>

Computational Processes. Evaluation, correction, learning, and optimization refine execution within the fixed constraint space established by the architecture. They improve execution without generating, replacing, or redefining identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-38">38</a></sup>

In this chapter, I have stated the Goal-Governed Model of Conceptual Identity. I have shown that conceptual identity depends on an upstream constraint structure rather than on downstream similarity, categorization, performance, or learning. I distinguished four architectural functions—conceptual identity, instantiation, categorization, and goal-directed evaluation—and separated them from the computational processes that execute them. By assigning each function its proper explanatory role, I resolved the apparent conflict between stability and variability within a single computational architecture.

From these invariants follows a final restriction: no downstream success condition alone constitutes a concept. Statistical convergence, predictive accuracy, cultural uptake, and practical utility evaluate execution under constraint. Each presupposes the identity conditions it evaluates.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-43">43</a></sup>

The Goal-Governed Model therefore defines the minimal computational architecture required for stability across variation, intelligible error, corrigible application, and computational implementation. Identity conditions specify admissibility. Instantiation realizes those conditions. Categorization organizes admissible instances. Goal-directed evaluation regulates them. Learning and optimization refine execution without redefining conceptual identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-44">44</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-45">45</a></sup>

This architecture preserves disciplined flexibility. Agents, cultures, and computational systems may apply different goals to admissible instances without multiplying concepts or revising their identity conditions. If the constraint structure changes, the concept changes. If the constraint remains and only execution varies, conceptual identity remains stable across variation.

The Goal-Governed Model explains the architecture of conceptual identity, but it does not yet explain how conceptual identity becomes meaning. In this chapter, I have explained how conceptual identity remains stable while execution varies, but architecture alone does not explain how concepts acquire semantic significance. Constraint structures specify what may count as an admissible instance. They do not yet explain how concepts come to stand for their referents, how signs acquire meaning, or how interpretation binds conceptual identity to reference.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-46">46</a></sup>

That is the task of Chapter 7. I now move from the architecture of conceptual identity to the architecture of meaning by developing the Constructivist Semiotics. There I explain how structured constraints become intelligible constructs and how interpretation transforms conceptual identity into semantic significance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-06.md#note-47">47</a></sup>
