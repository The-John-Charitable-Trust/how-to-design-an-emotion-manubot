# Appendix G: The Metric of Emotion

## Weighting and Justifying the Comparative Framework

G1. Appendix F established the candidate theories, frameworks, and models for the Chapter 9 comparative trade study. This appendix explains the methodology used to evaluate those candidates. I evaluate these candidates according to the architectural requirements that any coherent theory of emotional meaning must satisfy. The trade study therefore compares candidate theories by asking which one preserves the distinctions required for constructing emotional meaning (using Popper, Hempel, and Haack as methodological background).<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-g.md#note-1">1</a></sup>

I organize the methodology around four dependency-ordered evaluative domains (following Aristotle, Carnap, Peirce, and Marr) that follow directly from the governing principles I established earlier in this work. Part II, The Nature of Concepts, establishes the definitional and analytic principles that govern conceptual identity and truth. Part III, The Architecture of Meaning, establishes the cognitive-semiotic and computational principles that govern the construction, interpretation, and formal execution of meaning. I therefore evaluate every candidate according to those four domains: definitional adequacy, analytic coherence, cognitive-semiotic adequacy, and computational adequacy. These domains do not function as independent preferences. Each presupposes the successful completion of the preceding domain. A theory must first establish what emotion is before I can evaluate whether its propositions hold necessarily, whether it explains emotional meaning through interpretation, and whether it preserves those distinctions during formal execution.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-g.md#note-2">2</a></sup>

This dependency order follows the governing domains themselves. If a theory cannot satisfy the definitional domain by fixing what emotion is, I cannot meaningfully evaluate its analytic propositions because there is no stable concept to evaluate. If the theory cannot satisfy the analytic domain by establishing necessary and coherent propositions, I cannot determine whether it explains emotional meaning under the cognitive-semiotic domain. If the theory cannot satisfy the cognitive-semiotic domain by explaining how an agent constructs emotional meaning through interpretation, I cannot meaningfully test its computational adequacy because there is no coherent architecture to execute. Failure at any domain, therefore, terminates the evaluation because every subsequent domain presupposes the successful completion of the preceding one (a rule informed by Lakatos, Popper, and Haack on theory-evaluation).<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-g.md#note-3">3</a></sup>

Having established the methodological foundation of the comparative trade study, I now determine how each evaluative domain contributes to the overall assessment. Appendix H explains the macro-level weighting assigned to each governing domain together with the micro-level weighting assigned to the individual principles within those domains.
