# Chapter 13 — The Sound of Feelings

*Using Music to Model How We Make and Experience Emotion*

*Chapter 12 established the central distinction: affect prepares the body, feeling presents that preparation in consciousness, and emotion arises only when an agent interprets feeling under symbolic constraint. Chapter 13 asks what the ontology must still account for. I identify three problems: hedonic tone cannot explain the full qualitative character of feeling; emotion theory must distinguish affective mechanisms, interoceptive processing, feeling, and emotion; and emotional categories must explain how diverse instances can belong to the same kind without reducing that kind to biology, behavior, or essence.*

*I solve the first problem with timbre. Just as musical tone tells us what note sounds but cannot explain why a violin, piano, flute, and trumpet sound different, so hedonic tone tells us whether a feeling appears pleasant or unpleasant but cannot explain how that feeling appears in consciousness. I therefore introduce phenomenological timbre: the distinctive qualitative character through which feeling presents itself in first-person experience.*

*I solve the second problem with the instrument model. Just as an instrument supplies the mechanism that makes sound possible without becoming the sound or the composition, so affective mechanisms supply the bodily conditions that make feeling possible without becoming feeling or emotion. Interoception makes those bodily conditions available to consciousness. Feeling presents them with phenomenological timbre. Interpretation then constructs emotion from that felt experience.*

*I solve the third problem with the genre model. Just as a musical genre can include many performances without requiring identical instruments, notes, tempos, or arrangements, so an emotional category can include many interpreted instances without requiring one invariant physiological pattern, expression, or phenomenal quality. Emotional categories remain intelligible because agents organize diverse interpretations under rule-governed constraints.*

*Music therefore gives me more than an analogy. It gives me an explanatory architecture. Just as instrument, sound, timbre, composition, and genre occupy different roles in music, so affective mechanism, feeling, phenomenological timbre, emotion, and emotional category occupy different roles in the ontology of emotion. With this architecture in place, I can now move to Chapter 14, where the final question emerges: once emotion becomes meaningful, how does meaning become value?*

Chapter 12 established that emotion has a two-tier structure.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-1">1</a></sup> At the first tier, affective systems generate physiological states along the dimensions of valence, arousal, and motivational intensity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-2">2</a></sup> Interoceptive systems encode those states, and conscious awareness of that encoding produces feeling.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-3">3</a></sup> At the second tier, an agent interprets that experience under symbolic constraint and authorizes it as meaningful.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-4">4</a></sup> These conclusions leave three problems to resolve. First, hedonic tone cannot account for the qualitative diversity of experience. Second, we must distinguish affective mechanisms, interoceptive processing, feeling as conscious metaperception, and emotion as its interpretive construction. Third, we require a structural account that explains how emotions form categories of diverse cognitive–semiotic instances despite lacking a common essence.

I begin with the first problem. The qualitative character of feeling extends beyond hedonic tone. According to the Distinctive Feeling View, pleasure consists in a feeling with a distinctive hedonic tone.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-5">5</a></sup> The question is whether hedonic tone exhausts the qualitative structure of feeling. I argue that it does not. Hedonic tone captures only the phenomenal appearance of valence.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-6">6</a></sup> It does not account for the richer qualitative organization of conscious feeling. The limitation therefore lies not in the concept of hedonic tone itself, but in treating one phenomenal dimension as if it exhausted the whole of feeling.

Music confronts the same explanatory problem. A musical tone specifies measurable properties such as pitch, duration, and intensity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-7">7</a></sup> A violin, piano, flute, and trumpet may all produce the same note at the same loudness. Tone therefore identifies what is played, but it does not explain why each instrument sounds different.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-8">8</a></sup> Music resolves this limitation through the concept of timbre. Timbre refers to the characteristic qualitative colour of a sound that distinguishes one instrument from another despite identical pitch and intensity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-9">9</a></sup> Where tone specifies measurable dimensions, timbre explains qualitative character.

Feeling exhibits the same architecture. Hedonic tone captures only the phenomenal appearance of affective valence as pleasure or displeasure., Feeling, however, presents the organism with a richer qualitative experience than hedonic polarity alone can explain. Physiological affect varies not only in valence, but also in arousal, motivational intensity, salience, temporal organization, and other dimensions of bodily organization., If feeling consists in the conscious metaperceptual awareness of physiological affect, then its qualitative character must likewise extend beyond hedonic tone.

Music therefore resolves precisely the explanatory problem that the Distinctive Feeling View leaves unresolved. If music requires timbre to explain the qualitative character of sound beyond tone, then an adequate theory of feeling likewise requires an additional dimension to explain the qualitative character of conscious experience beyond hedonic tone.

I therefore introduce the concept of phenomenological timbre. Phenomenological timbre refers to the structured qualitative character of conscious feeling beyond hedonic tone. Just as musical timbre distinguishes sounds that share the same pitch and intensity, so phenomenological timbre distinguishes feelings that share similar hedonic polarity while differing in their qualitative character. Hedonic tone explains whether a feeling appears pleasant or unpleasant. Phenomenological timbre explains how that feeling appears in conscious experience.

I now refine the definition developed in Chapter 12. Feeling is the conscious metaperceptual awareness of interoceptively available physiological affect, presented in first-person experience with a distinctive phenomenological timbre, prior to detachable symbolic stabilization as emotion. The introduction of phenomenological timbre therefore resolves the first problem by accounting for the qualitative character of feeling beyond hedonic tone.

A second problem nevertheless remains. We must now determine how physiological affect, interoceptive processing, feeling, and emotion relate within a single explanatory architecture. Existing theories frequently assign explanatory priority to one level while neglecting another.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-10">10</a></sup> Rather than redefining these phenomena, I assign each to its proper explanatory level.

Music again provides the methodological framework. I develop four corresponding thought experiments. Each isolates a different explanatory level and shows why identifying emotion requires more than observing the mechanisms that precede it. Together they expose the same architectural mistake from four complementary perspectives.

A musical instrument produces the physical conditions necessary for sound, but no musician identifies a composition by examining the instrument alone.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-11">11</a></sup> The instrument constrains what a performer can produce, but it does not determine the composition. Likewise, physiology, anatomy, and neural activity generate the bodily conditions under which feeling becomes possible.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-12">12</a></sup> They do not determine emotional meaning. Researchers who identify emotion directly from physiological mechanisms therefore ask one explanatory level to perform the work of another.

The problem becomes even clearer when viewed through the philosophy of mind. The other minds problem reminds us that no observer possesses direct access to another person's conscious experience.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-13">13</a></sup> An observer may measure physiology with extraordinary precision, but physiology alone does not reveal phenomenology. Nor does phenomenology alone reveal emotional meaning. Emotion emerges only after an agent interprets experience under conceptual and contextual constraints.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-14">14</a></sup> An external observer therefore encounters successive explanatory boundaries rather than direct access to emotion itself.

Music illustrates these boundaries with unusual clarity. Imagine a profoundly deaf engineer who studies a violin. The engineer measures string tension, resonance, bow velocity, finger placement, and vibration with perfect accuracy. Despite possessing exhaustive knowledge of the instrument, the engineer cannot identify the composition because the engineer never encounters the sound itself. Complete knowledge of the mechanism does not produce knowledge of the phenomenon. The limitation does not arise from poor measurement. It arises because the engineer studies one explanatory level while the composition occupies another.

Behavior introduces the same limitation. A spectator may watch someone dance with remarkable precision. The spectator may describe posture, rhythm, timing, balance, and movement in extraordinary detail. Yet the spectator cannot identify the composition from the choreography alone. Different compositions support similar movements, and the same composition supports different choreography. The observer therefore infers rather than detects the music. Facial expression, posture, vocal tone, autonomic activity, and neural activation occupy the same epistemic position. They support inference about emotion without uniquely determining emotional identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-15">15</a></sup>

Modern music-recognition systems reinforce the same point. Applications such as Shazam identify musical compositions because they analyze the sound itself rather than the instrument that produces it, the musician who performs it, or the audience that responds to it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-16">16</a></sup> They operate at the explanatory level occupied by the composition's acoustic structure. Emotion research possesses no equivalent access. Researchers cannot analyze another person's phenomenological experience directly. Instead, they examine physiology, anatomy, neural activity, facial expression, or behavior. These observations reveal conditions that support emotion, but they do not constitute emotion itself.

Lisa Feldman Barrett's principle of degeneracy supports this analysis. Barrett argues that different neural and physiological organizations can instantiate the same emotional category rather than a single invariant biological pattern.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-17">17</a></sup> This principle corresponds closely to the musical model. Just as different instruments, ensembles, or orchestras can perform the same musical composition, different neural and physiological organizations can support the same interpreted emotion. Conversely, similar neural and physiological organization can support different emotional interpretations depending upon context, conceptual organization, and the agent's goals. Physiological affect therefore constrains emotional interpretation without uniquely determining emotional identity.

Researchers repeatedly commit two conceptual errors. First, they conflate explanatory levels by treating distinct processes as functionally interchangeable. Second, they collapse explanatory levels by allowing one level to perform the explanatory work of another.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-18">18</a></sup> John B. Watson explained emotional phenomena exclusively in terms of observable bodily responses and omitted qualitative experience altogether.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-19">19</a></sup> Fred Feldman's Distinctive Feeling View defines pleasure as a feeling with a distinctive hedonic tone and therefore gives explanatory priority to qualitative feeling.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-20">20</a></sup> Martha Nussbaum identifies emotions primarily with evaluative judgment and therefore gives explanatory priority to interpretation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-21">21</a></sup> Paul Ekman emphasizes recurring physiological and expressive organization.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-22">22</a></sup> Jaak Panksepp grounds emotional systems in evolutionarily conserved affective circuits.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-23">23</a></sup> Lisa Feldman Barrett explains emotions through the conceptual categorization of core affect.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-24">24</a></sup> Each theory illuminates an important explanatory level. None, by itself, explains the complete progression from physiological affect to phenomenological feeling to interpreted emotional meaning.

In this analysis, I resolve the second problem by assigning each phenomenon to its proper explanatory level. Physiological affect generates the bodily conditions of experience. Interoceptive processing makes those conditions available to consciousness. Feeling presents them as phenomenological experience characterized by phenomenological timbre. Emotion organizes that experience under conceptual and contextual constraints. Each level performs explanatory work that no other level can perform.

Music demands the same explanatory discipline. The instrument generates the sound. Timbre gives the sound its qualitative character. Composition organizes the sound into meaningful structure. No musicologist mistakes the instrument for the sound, the sound for the composition, or the composition for the instrument. Emotion requires the same discipline. Researchers should not ask physiological affect to perform the work of feeling, feeling to perform the work of interpretation, or interpretation to perform the work of physiological affect. Only after preserving these explanatory levels can I explain emotion without confusing physiological preparation, conscious experience, interpretive meaning, and emotional category.

I have now resolved the second problem by assigning physiological affect, interoceptive processing, feeling, and emotion to their proper explanatory levels. One problem nevertheless remains. I must still explain how emotional interpretations form coherent categories despite varying across contexts, cultures, individuals, and physiological realization. If no physiological mechanism, phenomenal quality, or conceptual feature uniquely determines emotional identity, what unifies an emotional category?

I return once more to music because it provides the final structural correspondence. A composition does not become a genre because every performance shares identical notes, instrumentation, tempo, or dynamics. Jazz remains jazz whether a solo pianist, a quartet, or a full orchestra performs it. Classical music remains classical despite enormous variation in instrumentation, phrasing, tempo, and interpretation. Genre therefore does not require identical instances. Instead, it organizes diverse compositions under a common structural identity while allowing substantial variation within the category.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-25">25</a></sup>

I propose that emotional categories exhibit the same architecture. No single physiological configuration, phenomenological timbre, behavioral expression, or conceptual feature determines membership in a category such as fear, grief, anger, or joy. Instead, agents organize individual emotional interpretations into recognizable categories that support communication, prediction, memory, and social coordination. The category does not arise from a shared essence. It arises from a structured pattern of admissible variation governed by conceptual constraints.

I can now explain why emotional categories remain both stable and flexible. Stability arises because agents repeatedly organize similar interpretations under shared conceptual constraints. Flexibility arises because no individual physiological state, phenomenological timbre, contextual feature, or behavioral expression fixes category membership. Musical genres tolerate variation without losing coherence. Emotional categories exhibit the same property. They organize families of related interpretations rather than collections of identical instances.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-26">26</a></sup>

Lisa Feldman Barrett's population thinking and principle of degeneracy support this conclusion. Barrett argues that emotional categories consist of diverse instances rather than invariant biological types. Different physiological organizations can therefore support the same emotional category, just as different instruments, ensembles, or orchestras can perform the same musical genre. Conversely, similar physiological organization can support different emotional interpretations depending upon context, conceptual organization, goals, and prior experience. Barrett's work therefore reinforces the conclusion that physiology constrains emotional interpretation without uniquely determining emotional identity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-27">27</a></sup>

I now complete the musical correspondence. The instrument corresponds to physiological affect. Sound corresponds to feeling. Timbre corresponds to phenomenological timbre. Composition corresponds to emotion as an interpreted instance. Genre corresponds to emotional category. Each level depends upon the preceding level while performing explanatory work that no preceding level can perform. No level replaces another. No level performs another's explanatory work.

I have now resolved the third problem. Emotional categories neither constitute natural kinds nor require fixed defining essences. They organize diverse interpreted experiences under rule-governed conceptual constraints in the same way that musical genres organize diverse compositions without requiring identical performances. The architecture therefore preserves both stability and variability without collapsing one into the other.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-28">28</a></sup>

I reject the assumption that emotional categories correspond to natural kinds or that emotional grammar forms a fixed, mind-independent system shared uniformly across agents. No single physiological organization, behavioral expression, phenomenological quality, or conceptual feature determines membership in a category such as fear or anger. Instead, agents construct emotional categories by organizing interpreted experiences into recognizable forms. Categories therefore reflect how agents stabilize meaning across instances rather than how the world presents a pre-given emotional taxonomy.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-29">29</a></sup>

This conclusion clarifies the role of grammar in emotional life. Affective organization constrains feeling, but emotional grammar organizes feeling into intelligible categories. Just as musical grammar organizes sound into compositions and genres, emotional grammar organizes phenomenological timbre into recognizable kinds. Grammar does not produce feeling. Physiological affect generates feeling through interoceptive processing. Emotional grammar organizes that feeling into recognizable categories and stabilizes its interpretation across instances.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-30">30</a></sup>

Empirical findings support this account. Studies of functional brain organization show that neural patterns associated with experience vary substantially across individuals and do not generalize cleanly without subject-specific transformation. Each individual exhibits a distinctive neural signature, and decoding approaches require individualized models to interpret neural activity accurately. Unlike music, which admits formal description through notes, pitch, rhythm, harmony, and compositional structure, affective experience possesses no comparable quantitative syntax. No accepted unit measures qualitative experience, and no formal grammar maps physiological affect to emotional categories with cross-agent invariance. Emotional grammar therefore operates as an agent-relative, constraint-governed system that stabilizes meaning without implying universality or reducibility to fixed structures.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-31">31</a></sup>

Appendix N develops this account further by examining the relationship between emotional grammar, mathematical realism, and constraint-governed meaning.

I began this chapter by identifying three architectural problems. I have now resolved each of them.

First, I argued that hedonic tone cannot account for the qualitative character of feeling. I resolved this problem by introducing phenomenological timbre. Just as musical timbre distinguishes the qualitative character of sounds that share the same pitch and intensity, so phenomenological timbre distinguishes the qualitative character of feelings that share similar hedonic polarity. Hedonic tone explains whether an experience feels pleasant or unpleasant. Phenomenological timbre explains how it feels.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-32">32</a></sup>

Second, I argued that existing theories blur or collapse the distinctions between physiological affect, interoceptive processing, feeling, and emotion. I resolved this problem by assigning each phenomenon to its proper explanatory level within a layered architecture. Just as a musical instrument generates the conditions for sound without becoming the composition, so physiological affect generates the bodily conditions of experience without becoming emotion. Just as sound precedes composition, so feeling precedes emotional interpretation. Each level performs explanatory work that no other level can perform.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-33">33</a></sup>

Third, I argued that emotional categories require an account of unity without shared essence. I resolved this problem by showing that emotional categories function like musical genres. Just as a musical genre organizes diverse compositions without requiring identical performances, so an emotional category organizes diverse interpreted experiences without requiring invariant physiological mechanisms, phenomenological qualities, or conceptual features. Categories preserve identity through structured constraint rather than shared essence.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-34">34</a></sup>

Music therefore serves not merely as an illustration, but as the methodological correspondence through which I derive the architecture of emotion. Just as musicians distinguish instrument from sound, sound from composition, and composition from genre, so I distinguish physiological affect from feeling, feeling from emotion, and emotion from emotional category. I preserve these distinctions because explanation depends upon them. Once I ask one explanatory level to perform the work of another, I cease to explain emotion and begin to confuse mechanism with experience, experience with meaning, and meaning with category. Only after preserving these distinctions can I explain emotion without confusing mechanism with experience, experience with meaning, meaning with category, or category with grammar.

One question nevertheless remains. Emotion no longer presents the deepest explanatory problem. Meaning does. If emotion consists in an interpreted, meaning-bearing construct, then I must now explain how agents evaluate that meaning, justify it, and coordinate it with others. In other words, I must move from the ontology of emotion to the ontology of value.

Music provides one final correspondence. An instrument produces sound. Timbre gives sound its qualitative character. Composition organizes sound into meaningful structure. Genre organizes compositions into recognizable families. None of these determines whether a composition is beautiful, elegant, profound, trivial, or morally significant. The listener must evaluate it. Meaning therefore precedes value. Value presupposes meaning without reducing to it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-35">35</a></sup>

Emotion follows the same architecture. Physiological affect prepares experience. Feeling presents experience. Interpretation constructs emotional meaning. Emotional meaning alone, however, does not determine what an agent ought to value, endorse, justify, or pursue. Interpretation therefore completes the ontology of emotion, but it does not complete the ontology of value.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-13.md#note-36">36</a></sup>

The final chapter completes that remaining task. I integrate the Constructivist Semiotic Sign, the Goal-Governed Constraint Model of Concepts, the Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions, and the Constructivist Computational Model of Emotions into a unified architecture of affect, feeling, emotion, meaning, and value. Having explained how emotion becomes meaningful, I now explain how meaning becomes value.
