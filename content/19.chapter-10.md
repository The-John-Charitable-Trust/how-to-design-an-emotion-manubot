# Chapter 10 — All I Know Is What I Have Symbols For

*The Linguistic Limits of Emotional Meaning*

*In this chapter, I move beneath emotion to the deeper architecture that makes emotional meaning possible at all. Chapter 9 showed that emotion does not live inside the body as a ready-made state. Emotion arises when an agent interprets feeling under goal, context, and constraint. Chapter 10 asks the next necessary question: what must a system possess before it can make that kind of meaning?*

*My answer begins with a hard distinction. Feeling is not meaning. A creature may feel, regulate, react, and adapt without yet producing emotional meaning in the strict sense. Meaning requires more than sensations, and phenomena. It requires symbols that a system can detach from immediate stimulus conditions, organize according to rules, and use to stabilize significance across contexts. Words count as symbols, but words do not exhaust the symbolic field. Gestures, diagrams, signs, musical notation, mathematical symbols, and internal identifiers can also function symbolically when a system uses them to organize meaning.*

*I argue that meaning requires structured representation interpreted relative to stake. Something must appear as something for a system for which it matters. This separates meaning from mere signal, reflex, and regulation. A thermostat can track a temperature difference, but it does not care. A body can activate under threat, but activation alone does not say what the activation means. Stake gives the system a normative orientation. Structure gives it form. Interpretation allows the system to use the representation to guide judgment, expectation, and response.*

*From there, I identify the symbolic thresholds that meaning requires. First, symbolic detachment allows an agent or system to invoke an identifier even when the original trigger is absent. Second, symbolic endorsement allows the system to proceed under that identifier as operative. Third, grammar allows symbols to produce significance with determinate structure. Fourth, metarepresentation allows the system to evaluate, revise, negate, or justify its own attributions. These thresholds turn raw affect and behavior into a genuine architecture of meaning.*

*This architecture also clarifies the difference between feeling and emotion. A feeling presents affect in first-person experience. It makes bodily life available to the subject, but it does not yet organize that life as structured significance. Emotion begins when an agent organizes feeling under a detachable symbolic identifier, conceptual constraint, context, and stake. Fear, shame, pride, embarrassment, anger, and betrayal do not simply occur as bodily facts. An agent stabilizes felt experience under identifiers that carry identity conditions and admit correction.*

*I call the domain-specific symbolic system of emotional meaning Emotionese. Emotionese names the generative architecture through which agents construct, stabilize, revise, and communicate emotional significance. It does not reduce emotion to spoken language. It names the broader symbolic system that allows affect to become intelligible as fear rather than excitement, shame rather than guilt, pride rather than relief. Without Emotionese, affect may occur, expression may occur, and regulation may occur. Emotional meaning does not yet occur.*

*The chapter’s governing thesis is therefore direct: the limits of my symbols are the limits of my meaning. In the emotional domain, the claim becomes sharper still: all I mean emotionally is what I have signs for. Where no detachable sign exists, no emotional meaning can arise in the strict architectural sense.*

*This conclusion does not deny feeling to animals or infants. It does not trivialize their affective lives. It raises the standard of the question. The issue is no longer whether animals and infants feel. They do. The issue is whether they possess the symbolic architecture required to transform feeling into detachable, endorsable, constraint-governed emotional meaning.*

*Chapter 11 applies that test. I now turn from the architecture of meaning to its most difficult cases: nonhuman animals and neurotypical infants. The question will not be whether they react, regulate, or suffer. The question will be whether they signify.*

Do animals have emotions? Do babies have emotions?

In Chapter 9, I established that emotion, in the strict sense developed here, is not physiological affect, not phenomenological feeling, and not an automatic appraisal output. Emotion is a goal-governed interpretive act through which an agent assigns significance to a felt state under conditions of context, constraint, and possible correction. Emotional meaning, therefore, is not discovered inside the body. An agent, for whom something is at stake, constructs emotion when they interpret a semiotically available feeling.

That account clarifies the nature of emotional meaning, but it also exposes a deeper requirement. If emotion is a constructed semiotic achievement by an agent, then I must now ask what makes semiotic achievement possible in the first place. Chapter 9 showed where emotional meaning arises in the architecture. Chapter 10 moves beneath that achievement to examine the symbolic conditions that allow any meaning, emotional or otherwise, to stabilize.

I therefore cannot answer the questions about animals and infants, simply by observing arousal, expression, or feeling alone. Those phenomena matter, but they do not settle the issue. The decisive question is whether a system possesses the symbolic architecture required to convert affective life into structured, detachable, and interpretable meaning. A creature may feel. It may regulate. It may respond intelligently to the world. But the question at issue is whether it can mean in the strict architectural sense required by the constructivist semiotic model of emotions.

I therefore begin upstream from emotion without abandoning emotion. I ask what must be present for something to count as meaning rather than mere sensation, signal, regulation, or behavioral coordination. Only after I define meaning in its structural forms can I specify the threshold for emotional meaning in animals and infants. Only then can I determine whether infants use symbols to stabilize feeling and ascribe it to themselves as content, and whether animal affect rises beyond structured regulation into detachable, endorsable emotion in the strict architectural sense.

Ludwig Wittgenstein famously wrote that “the limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-1">1</a></sup> The thought is often paraphrased, somewhat misleadingly,  as the claim that one can only know what one has words for. My concern, however, is not knowledge but meaning. I am not delimiting what can be known; I am specifying what can count as structured significance within a system.

Ferdinand de Saussure distinguished between langue, the rule-governed system, and parole, its enactment in speech.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-2">2</a></sup> The present thesis extends beyond both parole and lexical vocabulary. In the present framework, words count as symbols, but they do not exhaust the category of symbols. Marks, gestures, diagrams, mathematical signs, musical notation, and other rule-governed forms may also function symbolically when a system can detach them from immediate stimulus conditions and use them to organize meaning. What limits meaning, then, is not necessarily uttered words, but the availability of detachable symbolic architecture itself. The limits of my symbols determine the limits of my meaning. In this structural sense, all I mean is what I have symbols for.

Within the present framework, I advance the claim that meaning in the full semiotic sense requires detachable symbolic architecture. Where there are no detachable symbols, there can be no grammar in the strict combinatorial sense defined here. Where there is no grammar, structured significance cannot arise. Where there is no structured significance, there can be no generative language in any modality, and therefore no emotional meaning in the strict sense.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-3">3</a></sup>

On this foundation, I will specify the minimal semiotic constituents required for meaning and determine which are dispensable for proto-meaning. Only then can we answer, with precision rather than intuition: Do animals and babies meet the structural threshold for emotional meaning?

In this chapter, I treat emotional meaning as a problem in semiotic architecture rather than psychology or affective neuroscience. Instead of debating behavioral evidence or neural correlates, I identify the structural conditions that make meaning possible at all. I integrate philosophy of language, semiotics, cognitive architecture, and formal computational systems into a unified evaluative framework.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-4">4</a></sup> By specifying these thresholds explicitly, I replace anthropomorphic projection with principled criteria.

Before I ask whether animals or babies possess emotional meaning, I must clarify what meaning itself requires. The word “meaning” is used ambiguously. It often collapses fundamentally different dimensions under a single label. To avoid confusion, I must distinguish three axes along which the term shifts.

To maintain a rigorous standard for meaning, we must recognize it as an architectural achievement rather than a vague experiential glow. This requires a precise separation of the three axes along which meaning shifts, avoiding the ambiguities of folk psychology.

First, the phenomenological axis: sensation versus representation. A physiological event is a bodily change. Sensation is the felt awareness of such change, the “something it is like” to be in a state.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-5">5</a></sup> But sensation does not yet stabilize representational content. It presents experience without organizing it under conceptual constraint. Representation advances beyond immediacy by presenting something as something.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-6">6</a></sup> Meaning in the semiotic sense never consists in mere sensation; it requires structured representation.

Second, the normative axis: structure versus stake. Structure refers to a patterned organization governed by rules. A thermostat detects temperature differentials under structural constraint and regulates behavior relative to a set point, yet it does so without intrinsic stake. Its function is externally assigned by design; nothing is at risk for the system itself.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-7">7</a></sup>

Stake introduces normative orientation: the system treats something as mattering because it can gain it, preserve it, or lose it through its own continued operation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-8">8</a></sup> This requires that the system’s continued operation, integrity, or evaluative state is internally at issue. Structure without stake is blind computation. Stake without structure reduces to undifferentiated impulse. Meaning requires their integration.

Third, the interpretive axis: representation versus interpretation. A representation may exist within a system while remaining inert. Interpretation introduces uptake. The system proceeds under the representation as action-guiding, integrating it into expectation, choice, and outcome sensitivity.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-9">9</a></sup> Without interpretive integration, representations remain correlations within processing architecture.

Taken together, these axes yield a minimal criterion. Meaning arises when a state functions as a structured representation interpreted relative to stake within a system capable of integration and response. It is not mere sensation, not mere structure, and not mere causal linkage. It is structured significance: something presented as something for a system for which it matters.

Extensional classification can occur without fully intensional, identity-condition-governed meaning. A system may reliably sort patterns as threat, food, or ally through learned regularities while lacking identity conditions that fix what makes those classifications correct across contexts.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-10">10</a></sup> Extensional success therefore does not imply detachable, endorsable, constraint-governed significance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-11">11</a></sup> This distinction will matter when I evaluate whether animals and infants are eligible for emotional meaning.

I now preview the threshold claim that governs the rest of this chapter and the evaluation in Chapter 11, Do Animals and Infants Have Emotions? I do not view emotional meaning as a binary property that appears whenever affect is present. It is an architectural achievement that emerges only when a system crosses specific structural thresholds. I identify four thresholds as minimally required.

First, I require symbolic detachment. This is the capacity to invoke an identifier independently of its triggering instance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-12">12</a></sup> Second, I require symbolic endorsement. This is the integration of that identifier as action-guiding under its specific content.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-13">13</a></sup> Third, I require grammatical organization. These are the rule-governed constraints that relate identifiers into structured significance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-14">14</a></sup> Finally, I require metarepresentation. This is the capacity to treat one’s own attributions as attributions, which enables evaluation, revision, negation, and justification.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-15">15</a></sup>

In the rest of this chapter, I  specify these thresholds precisely. In Chapter 11, I apply them to two specific cases. I evaluate nonhuman animals as a species-typical class and neurotypical infants as a developmental ascent.

In the emotional domain, this generative symbolic architecture takes a specific form. I will later refer to it as Emotionese: the rule-governed system through which agents stabilize, organize, and communicate emotional meaning. For now, I will leave the development of the term for later. First, I must specify the general architecture of meaning that makes any such system possible.

I move beyond the blind robot of behavioral observation to identify the objective necessity of these structural constituents. I will use “meaning” in this strict architectural sense throughout: structured representation interpreted relative to stake.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-16">16</a></sup>

If meaning requires structured representation interpreted relative to stake, then the next question becomes unavoidable: what kind of system is capable of producing such structured representation?

Language, in the present architectural sense, is not restricted to spoken or written words. It refers to a generative symbolic architecture capable of producing structured significance. A language, in this broader sense, consists of discrete combinable units organized by rule into higher-order forms that admit interpretation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-17">17</a></sup>

This view aligns with the tradition represented by Jerry Fodor and Ray Jackendoff. Fodor argues that cognition operates over symbolically structured expressions with combinatorial and compositional properties.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-18">18</a></sup> Jackendoff similarly maintains that linguistic competence reflects parallel representational structures linking form and conceptual organization.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-19">19</a></sup> What defines language, on this account, is not vocalization but rule-governed symbolic structure capable of generating indefinitely many meaningful combinations.

Natural language is  one paradigmatic instantiation of this architecture. Mathematics, music, and formal computational systems likewise organize discrete units under combinatorial constraint. In each domain, hierarchical organization enables structured significance to emerge from minimal symbolic primitives.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-20">20</a></sup>

| Domain | Minimal Unit | Intermediate Structure | Higher-Order Structure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Natural Language | Phoneme / Character | Word / Clause | Proposition |
| Mathematics | Symbol / Variable | Expression / Equation | Theorem / Formal Statement |
| Music | Note | Motif / Phrase | Thematic or Expressive Structure |
| Formal Systems | Token / Symbol | Statement / Instruction | Executable Program or Proof |

<p align="center">**Table 10-1. Hierarchical Organization of Meaning Across Symbolic Domains**</p>
Table 10-1 illustrates a common structural pattern. Discrete elements do not merely accumulate; they are organized under rule into increasingly complex configurations that admit interpretation. Where such generative structure operates, symbols can function as components of structured meaning. Where it does not, tokens fail to enter relations with one another, and significance cannot stabilize.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-21">21</a></sup>

If language produces structured significance, I must now ask what enables it to organize symbols into such structure.

If language is a generative symbolic architecture, grammar is the principle that makes that architecture productive. Grammar is the rule-governed combinatorial organization of symbols into structured forms capable of carrying significance. It is not merely a catalog of permissible expressions, but a system of constraints that determines how an interpreter may combine, order, and relate discrete units.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-22">22</a></sup>

Grammar operates by governing admissibility. It specifies which symbol combinations a system accepts as valid and which it rejects. In natural language, grammar regulates the arrangement of words into phrases and clauses. In mathematics, it governs the formation of expressions and equations. In music, it constrains harmonic progression, rhythmic organization, and thematic development. In formal systems, it determines which sequences of tokens constitute valid statements or executable instructions. In each case, grammar enables an interpreter to transform separate symbols into organized structures and assign them meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-23">23</a></sup>

Structured significance emerges at this level. A single word such as “Danger” may already function as structured significance when it operates within a grammatical system that differentiates it from other possible symbols and situates it within a broader network of relations.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-24">24</a></sup> Likewise, a musical motif may carry expressive force not because it forms a proposition, but because a rule-governed harmonic and rhythmic framework organizes it. Grammar therefore enables significance without requiring full predication.

Predication, the explicit subject–predicate form of “X is Y,” is one powerful grammatical construction, but it is not the only way structured significance can arise.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-25">25</a></sup> “The apple is red” displays full propositional form. Yet an imperative such as “Stop,” a warning like “Fire,” or an exclamation such as “Danger!” can function meaningfully without conforming to complete propositional syntax. What matters is not the presence of a full sentence form, but the presence of rule-governed combinatorial organization that differentiates and stabilizes symbolic relations. Even the single-word warning “Fire” presupposes a structured relation among speaker, context, and possible action.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-26">26</a></sup>

Grammar marks the transition from mere symbolic presence to structured significance. A list of isolated words such as “apple, red, table” carries no determinate meaning. Once grammar organizes them into “The red apple is on the table,” relations stabilize and grammatical structure constrains interpretation. Symbols become interpretable in relation to one another under rule-governed structure. Without grammar, symbols remain inert tokens. Grammar introduces relational constraint, allowing identifiers to enter structured configurations.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-27">27</a></sup> At this point, the ascending levels of semiotic organization can be stated with precision.

A detachable identifier marks the first shift. Once stabilized under identity conditions and invocable beyond its immediate trigger, a reusable symbolic type exists.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-28">28</a></sup> Yet detachment alone does not yield structured meaning. The system must proceed under the identifier as operative. Endorsement occurs when the symbol functions as action-guiding within the field of alternatives.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-29">29</a></sup>

When an agent endorses an identifier and places it into relational organization under grammatical constraint, structured significance emerges. In a threat case, content such as hawk–danger–here is no longer a mere activation but a directional configuration linking predator type, threat value, and spatial immediacy. The structure encodes content without yet articulating full predication.

Propositional form advances this structure into explicit assertion. “There is a hawk overhead” distinguishes subject and predicate and introduces truth-evaluable content. The representation now admits negation, embedding, and counterfactual projection. Correctness conditions become explicit rather than implicit.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-30">30</a></sup>

Metarepresentation introduces a further transformation. The system represents its own representation as representation. It can assess, revise, or justify its attribution: “I thought there was a hawk, but I was mistaken.” The content itself becomes an object of evaluation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-31">31</a></sup>

Detachment enables portability. Endorsement enables uptake. Grammar enables relational structure. Propositional form enables explicit correctness conditions. Metarepresentation enables reflective revision. Emotional meaning must be located somewhere along this progression. To determine where, we must now specify the minimal constituents of a semiotic sign.

In Chapter 7: The Constructivist Semiotics: The Architecture of Meaning, I introduced the formal structure of meaning as a Constructivist Semiotic Sign (CSS). The goal was to move beyond the traditional dyadic and triadic models of Ferdinand de Saussure and Charles Sanders Peirce and articulate the full micro-architecture required for semiotic meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-32">32</a></sup> Meaning, on this account, is not merely correlation or convention. It is a structured configuration composed of distinct but interdependent constituents.

The first constituent is the Identifier. The identifier is the detachable symbolic token. It may be linguistic, mathematical, musical, gestural, or internal. What defines it is not its modality but its detachment. It must be reusable across contexts and invocable in the absence of its instance. Without detachment, we remain at the level of signal.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-33">33</a></sup>

The second constituent is the Construct. The construct is the instantiated configuration organized under the identifier. It is the structured representation that arises when features are grouped according to constraint. Constructs are not mere sensations. They are organized configurations stabilized by an identifier.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-34">34</a></sup>

The third constituent is the Concept. The concept provides the constraint structure that governs admissibility. It specifies what counts as a legitimate instantiation. Concepts are not fixed essences  but rule- governed constraint structures  that regulate the formation of constructs.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-35">35</a></sup>

The fourth constituent is the Referent. The referent is what the construct is about. It may be external, internal, abstract, imagined, or absent. Importantly, referential presence is not required for proto-meaning. A construct may be internally organized without successfully referring to an external state.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-36">36</a></sup>

The fifth constituent is the Interpreter. The interpreter is the agent-relative system for whom the configuration matters. Without an interpreter, there is no stake, and therefore no meaning in the full semiotic sense.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-37">37</a></sup>

The sixth constituent is Interpretation. Interpretation is the act or process through which the interpreter organizes a construct under a concept relative to stake. Interpretation introduces correctness conditions and the possibility of error.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-38">38</a></sup>

These constituents do not all appear at every level of the ladder.

At the level of signal, an identifier may track an instance and possibly a referent, but no concept governs the construct, and the system has not detached the identifier from the immediate case. Interpretation remains regulatory rather than semiotic.

At the level of proto-meaning, we require a detachable identifier, a construct that conceptual constraints organize, and an interpreter. A referent is not essential. Interpretation occurs, but it may lack full propositional articulation or metarepresentational endorsement.

At the level of full CSS propositional meaning, all constituents are operative. The identifier is detachable and grammatically combinable. Conceptual constraint governs the construct. The referent may be external or abstract. The interpreter not only organizes the construct but can evaluate and revise it. Metarepresentation becomes possible: the system can represent its own representation as representation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-39">39</a></sup> With the constituents specified, we can now locate feeling and emotion within this architecture.

Constructivist Semiotics does not emerge in isolation. It stands at the intersection of several major traditions, integrating their strongest insights while extending them into a unified procedural account of meaning.

Ludwig Wittgenstein shows that language structures the limits of what one can mean.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-40">40</a></sup> We accept this insight but generalize it. Words are only one species of symbolic architecture. Meaning depends not merely on linguistic vocabulary but on the availability of symbols capable of structured recombination. In this broader sense, the limits of my symbols determine the limits of my meaning.

Ayn Rand argues that concepts depend on definitions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-41">41</a></sup> We preserve that insight while situating it within semiotic structure. Definitions clarify conceptual constraint, but meaning arises only when a concept operates within a fully activated sign. Conceptual structure alone does not generate understanding; an interpreter must coordinate it with identifier, construct, context, and stake.

Lisa Feldman Barrett demonstrates that emotion concepts are necessary for the construction of emotions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-42">42</a></sup> We extend this dependency downward and upward. Concepts require symbolic primitives in order to exist, and emotional meaning requires more than concepts alone. It requires the activation of the entire macro-semiotic configuration.

Ferdinand de Saussure described pre-linguistic thought and sound as a “nebulous mass” that a system of differences must differentiate before language can produce meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-43">43</a></sup> This insight implies that meaningful differentiation requires internal segmentation rather than mere flux. Jerry Fodor and Ray Jackendoff defend an internal symbolic system, often termed mentalese, that supplies the substrate for such differentiation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-44">44</a></sup> Their work secures the micro-semiotic requirement that representation presupposes symbolic tokens rather than associative linkage alone. Constructivist Semiotics integrates these insights by showing how symbolic primitives provide the internal segmentation that escapes the nebulous mass and scales upward into grammar, structured significance, and full sign formation.

These traditions are not competing theories but partial descriptions of a deeper unity. Constructivist Semiotics synthesizes them into a single procedural model: symbols are necessary at the micro-semiotic level; fully activated signs are necessary at the macro level. Meaning is not stimulus, not sensation, not mere concept, but structured semiotic organization under conditions of stake.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-45">45</a></sup>

This integrated architecture now provides the evaluative foundation required to revisit feeling and emotion with precision rather than intuition.

In Chapter 9, The Constructivist Semiotics Model of Emotion, I provisionally defined feeling as the conscious metaperceptual awareness of a semiotically available phenomenological experience, presented in first-person experience with a distinctive affective character, prior to interpretation, evaluation, or the assignment of meaning. That formulation remains fundamentally correct, but I must now synchronize it with this chapter’s architectural account of meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-46">46</a></sup>

A feeling is not identical to bodily sensation itself, nor is it an emotional judgment. The subject phenomenologically registers physiological affect as feeling. This registration makes affect consciously available to the agent without yet organizing it into structured significance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-47">47</a></sup> In architectural terms, feeling occupies the level of conscious semiotic availability. It may supply material for symbolic organization, but it does not itself contain a detachable identifier, conceptual constraint, grammatical relation, or proto-propositional form. Availability is not activation. A feeling can enter interpretation, but an agent has not yet interpreted it. It presents affect as lived immediacy, not as structured meaning.

This distinction is decisive. Conceptual constraint does not yet govern feeling as a construct. Feeling does not operate under a detachable symbolic identifier. It does not participate in grammar or proto-propositional organization. It is therefore pre-interpretive. It supplies the phenomenological material from which an agent may construct emotional meaning, but it is not itself emotional meaning. Feeling therefore precedes the semiotic thresholds; it remains semiotically available affective experience prior to detachable symbolic stabilization as emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-48">48</a></sup>

In Chapter 9, I defined emotion as a goal-directed, context-sensitive interpretive act through which an agent assigns significance to a felt phenomenological experience under conditions that admit correctness, error, justification, and revision. That definition must now be reformulated in explicitly architectural terms.

Emotion is a goal-directed, context-sensitive semiotic interpretive act through which an agent organizes feeling into proto-propositional meaning under a detachable symbolic identifier governed by conceptual constraint and situated relative to stake. Emotion does not merely register feeling; it structures feeling. The agent stabilizes the felt experience under a symbol, constrains it by identity conditions, and integrates it into a system capable of normative assessment.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-49">49</a></sup>

This clarification yields three consequences.

First, emotion requires structured representation. It is not reducible to physiological affect, phenomenological feeling, behavioral expression, regulatory modulation, or mere classification.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-50">50</a></sup> Those may function as inputs, correlates, or components. Emotion occurs only when an agent organizes feeling into a semiotic construct under a detachable identifier governed by conceptual constraint.

Second, emotion introduces correctness conditions. Because the construct operates under identity conditions and contextual constraint, it may be appropriate or inappropriate, justified or mistaken. The possibility of revision presupposes at least proto-propositional organization.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-51">51</a></sup>

Third, emotion operates at minimum at the proto-propositional level. It need not exhibit explicit subject-predicate articulation, but it must manifest structured significance beyond phenomenological feeling. Metarepresentational interpretation marks the reflective form of emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-52">52</a></sup>

The distinction between feeling and emotion is therefore architectural rather than merely psychological. Feeling is conscious affective presentation prior to detachable symbolic stabilization. Emotion is the structured semiotic organization of that presentation under a detachable symbol.

Many contemporary theories equate emotion with affective circuitry, appraisal dynamics, expressive behavior, or regulatory modulation. Affective neuroscience often locates emotion in subcortical systems.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-53">53</a></sup> Developmental psychology attributes emotions to infants based on patterned facial expression and goal-directed distress.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-54">54</a></sup> Minimalist theories treat emotion as any valenced appraisal that guides action.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-55">55</a></sup> Within the present architectural framework, I argue that these equivalences are not sufficient for emotional meaning. Physiological activation, appraisal processes, and expressive patterns may be necessary components or correlates of emotion. They are not sufficient for emotional meaning. Emotion, in the strict sense I defend, requires semiotic organization under detachable symbolic identifiers governed by identity conditions. Without that architecture, there may be affect, regulation, and evaluation. There is not yet emotion as structured meaning.

Having defined emotional meaning in architectural terms, I can now identify the decisive threshold condition that determines whether such organization is present at all.

If emotional meaning requires symbolic detachment, endorsement, grammatical organization, and metarepresentational capacity, then a domain-specific symbolic system must realize it. Just as mathematics depends upon notation and rule-governed transformation, and music depends upon tonal grammar, emotional meaning depends upon its own generative architecture. I refer to this architecture as Emotionese.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-56">56</a></sup>

Emotionese is the symbolic system through which agents construct, stabilize, and communicate emotional meaning. It is not reducible to spoken language. Its grammar consists in rule-governed relations among detachable emotional identifiers, their objects, contextual parameters, and stake. Identifiers such as fear, shame, pride, or betrayal function as symbolic primitives that organize affect under conceptual constraint.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-57">57</a></sup>

Emotionese does not depend exclusively on verbal labels. Facial configurations, vocal tone, posture, and physiological cues may contribute as properties within a construct. Their significance derives not from their physical form but from their organization within the semiotic architecture. A tightened jaw is a muscular contraction. It becomes anger only when an agent stabilizes it under an identifier governed by identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-58">58</a></sup>

Emotionese functions within shared normative frameworks. Shared does not imply uniformity, but cultural stabilization of prototypes that coordinate interpretation. These prototypes operate as constraint structures that guide admissible instantiation. Members of a community do not merely detect inner entities; they participate in a rule-governed symbolic system that organizes affect into structured significance.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-59">59</a></sup>

Emotionese therefore names the generative infrastructure of emotional meaning. Where such architecture is absent, there may be affect, expression, and regulation. Where it is present, affect can be organized into structured semiotic content. Emotional meaning emerges only within this system.

With the structure of Emotionese now clear, I can specify the decisive threshold condition that separates signaling from semiotic emotional meaning.

The Constructivist Semiotic Sign (CSS) formalizes this architecture by specifying the constituents required for intensional meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-60">60</a></sup> Only when all constituents of the CSS function together does intensional meaning arise. When one drops out, meaning correspondingly drops in kind and depth. Perception may persist. Sensation may persist. Behavior may persist. But understanding no longer follows in the full semiotic sense.

Lisa Feldman Barrett employs three recurring examples, the red apple, the falling tree, and the unfamiliar gamelan, to demonstrate that perception depends upon concepts.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-61">61</a></sup> Her aim is not merely to deny naïve realism, but to show that the brain uses learned conceptual categories to organize sensory input into determinate experience. Color perception requires color concepts. Sound perception requires auditory concepts. Musical experience requires musical concepts. In her broader theory, emotion likewise requires emotion concepts to organize affect into instances such as fear or anger.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-62">62</a></sup>

I accept this conceptual dependence and extend it. Concepts are necessary for construction, but they are not sufficient for full semiotic meaning. For structured significance to arise, concepts must operate within a detachable symbolic architecture governed by identity conditions and combinatorial constraint. Full meaning requires activation of the complete Constructivist Semiotic Sign: a detachable identifier, intensional constraint, a structured construct, a referent situated in context, and an interpreter for whom something is at stake.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-63">63</a></sup>

Barrett’s examples of the red apple, the falling tree, and unfamiliar gamelan music illustrate this structure. In each case, sensory input alone does not generate meaning. Meaning arises only when a detachable identifier operates under intensional identity conditions, organizes a construct relative to a referent-in-context, and is endorsed by an interpreter for whom the distinction matters. The full formal walkthrough of these cases is provided in Appendix M. Here I move directly to the emotional domain, where the same architecture governs the transition from affect to emotional meaning.

Consider now the emotional meaning. Suppose an individual stands before an audience and experiences increased heart rate, muscle tension, narrowed attention, and anticipatory imagery of failure. The referent-in-context is this specific social situation: public speaking before evaluative others where reputation and status are at stake.

Physiological activation and phenomenological feeling are present. But affect alone does not yet constitute emotional meaning. Emotional meaning arises only when an agent applies the concept FEAR under its intensional identity conditions. Those identity conditions specify structural constraints such as perceived threat, anticipated harm, vulnerability relative to stake, and future-oriented negative possibility. They differentiate FEAR from excitement, shame, or anger.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-64">64</a></sup>

The construct organizes the felt state under those governing constraints. It integrates bodily arousal, imagined outcome, evaluative appraisal, and situational context into a structured configuration such as “threat to social standing.” The detachable identifier “fear” stabilizes this construct. Because the identifier operates under conceptual constraint, it is endorsable. The interpreter can assert, “I am afraid of failing,” or revise the construct as, “This is excitement, not fear.” Correctness, error, justification, and revision become possible because the concept fixes admissibility conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-65">65</a></sup>

The interpreter-with-stake is the agent whose reputation, self-concept, or goal attainment matters. Stake grounds evaluation. Without stake, the construct lacks normative orientation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-66">66</a></sup>

Full emotional meaning therefore requires coordination of all constituents: a detachable and endorsable identifier such as “fear,” a concept specifying intensional identity conditions, a construct organizing felt affect under those constraints, a referent-in-context defining the social situation, and an interpreter for whom the outcome matters.

Without detachable symbolic stabilization under conceptual constraint, there may be physiological affect, phenomenological feeling, behavioral expression, or regulatory response. But there is no emotion in the strict semiotic sense. Emotional meaning emerges only when affect is organized into structured significance under a detachable and endorsable identifier within the full Constructivist Semiotic architecture.

This yields the governing thesis of the chapter. All we mean intensionally, in the strict architectural sense I have developed here, is what we have symbols for. The limits of my symbols are the limits of my meaning. In the emotional domain the principle is exact. All we mean emotionally is what we have signs for. Where no sign is available, no emotional meaning can arise.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-67">67</a></sup>

Within this architecture, the decisive condition is symbolic detachment. A detachable symbolic identifier is a reusable token that can be invoked independent of its immediate instance and stabilized under conceptual constraint. Detachment makes displacement possible. Symbolic endorsement organizes a felt state under such an identifier, producing proto-propositional structure. Metarepresentational awareness adds the capacity to evaluate, revise, negate, or justify that organization, introducing correctness conditions in the strict sense.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-68">68</a></sup>

I can therefore state the threshold once, cleanly, and carry it forward into the next chapter. Emotional meaning exists if and only if, within this model, an interpreter with stake uses a detachable symbolic identifier, applies its identity conditions, instantiates a construct within context, and endorses the felt state as emotionally meaningful. Full propositional meaning further requires metarepresentational awareness sufficient for explicit evaluation and revision.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-69">69</a></sup>

Consider a student who receives critical feedback on an essay. Physiological activation and discomfort arise. This is affect and feeling, but not yet emotional meaning. When the student stabilizes the state under the identifier “embarrassment” and organizes it into the construct “others are judging my competence,” endorsement occurs. Emotional meaning is present. If the student then reflects, “Perhaps this is constructive criticism rather than humiliation,” the representation becomes an object of evaluation. Metarepresentation has entered. Correctness conditions now operate.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-70">70</a></sup>

My argument yields explicit threshold conditions. I identify symbolic detachment as the first decisive shift. An identifier must be invocable across contexts, even when its triggering instance is absent.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-71">71</a></sup> I see symbolic endorsement as the next shift. The system must proceed under the identifier as operative. It organizes behavior and expectation as if its content holds.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-72">72</a></sup> Grammar then supplies the relational admissibility that allows symbols to produce significance with determinate structure. Without grammar, symbols remain inert tokens.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-73">73</a></sup> Finally, I recognize that metarepresentational capacity introduces correctness conditions. The system can evaluate, revise, negate, or justify its own attributions as attributions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-74">74</a></sup>

Now that I have specified the architecture of meaning, I can state the remaining dispute with precision rather than intuition. The question is no longer whether animals and infants display affect, regulation, or patterned behavior. The question is whether they cross the symbolic thresholds that emotional meaning requires.

Do nonhuman animals possess detachable symbolic identifiers that identity conditions stabilize?<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-75">75</a></sup> Do neurotypical infants organize affect into constructs that such constraints govern?<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-76">76</a></sup> Do either proceed under those identifiers as action-guiding in a manner that constitutes endorsement?<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-77">77</a></sup> Do either demonstrate metarepresentational capacity sufficient to evaluate, revise, or negate their own attributions?<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-10.md#note-78">78</a></sup>

These are not rhetorical questions. They are architectural tests. Emotional meaning exists if and only if a system satisfies these conditions. In Chapter 11, I apply this threshold model directly. I evaluate nonhuman animals as a species-typical class and infants as a developmental ascent. The issue is not whether they feel. The issue is whether they signify in the strict semiotic sense I define here.
