# Appendix F: Selecting the Candidates

## Why These Emotion Theories Made the Cut

In this appendix, I explain why I included each candidate theory, framework, or model of emotion in the Chapter 9 comparative trade study. Inclusion does not imply endorsement. Instead, inclusion reflects architectural necessity. I include a candidate only when it contributes a distinct architectural strategy for explaining affect, emotion, meaning, agency, or computation. Each candidate therefore functions either as an architectural contributor, a limiting case, or a source of explanatory requirements evaluated under the governing principles I developed in the preceding chapters.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-1">1</a></sup>

I select candidates according to their architectural relevance rather than their empirical popularity, historical prominence, or applied utility. A candidate qualifies only if it contributes a distinct architectural requirement that the trade study must either preserve, evaluate, or reject during the construction of the Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-2">2</a></sup>

I. Candidates Explicitly Addressing Meaning, Evaluation, or Construction

1. Appraisal Theories (Richard S. Lazarus, 1922–2002; Klaus R. Scherer, 1943– ; Ira J. Roseman, 1952– )

Appraisal theories explicitly treat emotions as about something. They explain emotion through the evaluation of relevance, consequence, action possibility, and personal significance. Unlike many biological or behavioral accounts, appraisal theories recognize that emotions involve judgments, whether implicit or explicit, about situations in relation to an organism's goals.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-3">3</a></sup>

For that reason, appraisal theories become unavoidable candidates under any framework that treats emotional meaning as central rather than incidental. They also introduce early accounts of error, misappraisal, and revision, recognizing that emotional evaluation can succeed or fail rather than merely occur. These commitments make appraisal theories indispensable for evaluating the analytic principles governing correctness conditions and the cognitive-semiotic principles governing contextual significance, goal-directedness, and reinterpretation, even though they ultimately stop short of explaining how appraisal becomes interpretation.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-4">4</a></sup>

2. Component Process Model (CPM - Scherer)

Scherer's Component Process Model (CPM) provides one of contemporary psychology's most architecturally explicit accounts of emotion. CPM organizes an emotional episode into an explicit temporal progression in which evaluation gives rise to physiological change, physiological change gives rise to feeling, and these processes subsequently coordinate expression and action readiness. This explicit phase separation makes CPM especially valuable for the present trade study because it allows me to evaluate whether each phase preserves its own explanatory role while remaining distinct from the others.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-5">5</a></sup> <sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-6">6</a></sup>

3. Theory of Constructed Emotion (TCE) / Conceptual Act Theory (CAT) (Lisa Feldman Barrett, 1963– )

Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion, also known as Conceptual Act Theory, offers the most explicit contemporary attempt to integrate affect, concepts, categorization, prediction, and social learning into a unified constructionist account of emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-7">7</a></sup>

TCE directly addresses concept, categorization, and construction, which places it at the center of this project's concerns. I therefore treat it as a necessary point of confrontation. It helps me preserve what constructivism gets right about variability, learning, and contextual interpretation, while also testing whether construction can preserve the formal difference between concept and instance, identifier and referent, interpreter and process.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-8">8</a></sup>

Beyond Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion, I include psychological constructionism as a broader family of theories represented by Russell, Barrett, and Frijda. These approaches explain emotions as products of more general psychological operations, including affect, attention, memory, conceptualization, categorization, and perception, rather than as biologically discrete natural kinds.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-9">9</a></sup>

This broader family allows me to test whether construction preserves stable identity across variation rather than becoming arbitrary. In other words, can a constructivist theory explain how emotions differ from one another without treating every emotional episode as a separate invention? That question matters because a constructivist account must preserve identity across variation: fear must remain fear across different situations, bodies, histories, and contexts, even though no single bodily pattern or facial display defines it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-10">10</a></sup>

II. Candidates Grounding Physiological Affect as the Biological Substrate

5. Affective Neuroscience (Jaak Panksepp, 1943–2017)

Affective neuroscience provides one of the strongest biological accounts of physiological affect. Panksepp distinguishes primary affective systems from cognition, language, and culture, relating them to neural systems that organize seeking, fear, rage, care, panic, play, and desire.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-11">11</a></sup>

This contribution makes affective neuroscience indispensable to the trade study because it explains the biological substrate from which emotional meaning later arises. It allows me to distinguish physiological affect from phenomenological feeling and from cognitive-semiotic emotion rather than collapsing them into a single phenomenon. I therefore preserve physiological affect as the biological foundation of emotional life while evaluating whether additional cognitive-semiotic architecture becomes necessary before an agent constructs emotional meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-12">12</a></sup>

6. Predictive Processing / Active Inference Accounts of Emotion (Karl Friston, 1959–; Andy Clark, 1957–; Jakob Hohwy, 1971–)

Predictive processing and active inference accounts propose a unified computational framework for perception, action, affect, and cognition. Their formal mathematical architectures also make them especially suitable for comparison with philosophical and computational accounts of emotion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-13">13</a></sup>

I include these accounts because they extend that framework to meaning itself. This broader explanatory scope allows me to evaluate whether formal computation preserves semantic distinctions or instead assimilates emotional meaning to prediction, control, or error correction.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-14">14</a></sup>

III. Candidates Explaining Emotion Through Function, Regulation, or Behavior

7. Functionalist / Evolutionary Emotion Theories (Charles Darwin, 1809–1882; Nico H. Frijda, 1927–2015; Randolph M. Nesse, 1948– )

Functionalist and evolutionary theories explain affective mechanisms in terms of the adaptive problems they evolved to solve.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-15">15</a></sup> On this view, fear supports threat avoidance, anger supports confrontation or boundary defense, disgust supports contamination avoidance, and attachment supports bonding and care.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-16">16</a></sup> These accounts become important because many theorists treat adaptive function as sufficient to explain what an emotion is.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-17">17</a></sup>

However, adaptive function may explain why an affective mechanism exists or what behavior it promotes, but it does not by itself explain how an emotion acquires its semantic identity through interpretation by an agent in context.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-18">18</a></sup> I therefore include functionalist and evolutionary theories so the trade study can evaluate whether evolutionary utility fixes semantic identity or whether emotional meaning still requires interpretation by an agent in context.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-19">19</a></sup>

8. Regulatory Models of Emotion (Antonio R. Damasio, 1944–; James J. Gross, 1960–)

Regulatory models explain emotional phenomena through bodily regulation, homeostatic control, decision guidance, and strategies for modifying emotional response.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-20">20</a></sup> These approaches have strongly influenced neuroscience, psychology, and emotional intelligence research, making them necessary candidates for the present trade study.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-21">21</a></sup>

Regulatory models explain how organisms maintain, redirect, or modify their physiological and behavioral states, but regulation alone does not establish emotional meaning. Emotional meaning requires cognitive-semiotic interpretation by an agent in context.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-22">22</a></sup> The trade study therefore evaluates whether regulation preserves the identity of an emotion or instead reduces emotional meaning to regulatory mechanism.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-23">23</a></sup>

IV. Non-Qualifying Candidates and Boundary Conditions

9. Basic Emotion Theory (BET) (Silvan S. Tomkins, 1911–1991; Paul Ekman, 1934–2023; Carroll E. Izard, 1923–2017)

Basic Emotion Theory (BET) provides the strongest representative of natural-kind realism about emotion. BET proposes that emotions such as fear, anger, sadness, happiness, disgust, and surprise correspond to biologically discrete categories with characteristic expressions and universal signatures.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-24">24</a></sup>

I retain BET as a limiting case because it states its ontological commitments with unusual clarity. The trade study therefore tests whether biologically discrete kinds can satisfy the governing architectural criteria for emotional meaning. If they can, biological identity alone suffices to ground emotion. If they cannot, then the architecture requires additional explanatory elements beyond biological kinds, including interpretation, agency, and semantic constraint.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-25">25</a></sup>

10. Dimensional Models (James A. Russell, 1947–; Lisa Feldman Barrett, 1963– )

Dimensional models organize affective states along a small number of dimensions, most commonly valence and arousal. They provide useful tools for representing the body's physiological registration of affective intensity and hedonic tone, while also organizing reports of conscious feeling within the same dimensional space.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-26">26</a></sup>

The trade study therefore asks whether dimensional models preserve the distinction between physiological sensation and phenomenological perception or instead collapse both into a single affective representation. It also asks whether that representation can determine emotional meaning. A location within valence-arousal space may characterize physiological sensation, phenomenological feeling, or both, but it does not determine what the feeling means. The same physiological and phenomenological profile may support fear, anger, shame, grief, or relief depending upon concept, context, referent, identifier, interpreter, and goal.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-27">27</a></sup>

11. Behaviorist and Associative Accounts (John B. Watson, 1878–1958; Burrhus Frederic "B. F." Skinner, 1904–1990)

Behaviorist and associative accounts explain emotional phenomena through stimulus-response patterns, conditioning, reinforcement, and learned association.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-28">28</a></sup>

These theories serve as limiting cases because they remove interpretation from the architecture of emotional meaning altogether. Once emotion becomes response or association, the architecture can no longer explain semantic achievement, interpretive governance, or emotional meaning itself. The trade study therefore identifies behaviorism as the point at which a theory fails the definitional and cognitive-semiotic domains because it cannot satisfy the architectural requirements for emotional meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-f.md#note-29">29</a></sup>

This appendix establishes the candidate theories, frameworks, and models that enter the Chapter 9 trade study. Appendix G establishes the weighting methodology by explaining how I evaluate every candidate according to explicit architectural criteria rather than preference, familiarity, or theoretical prestige.
