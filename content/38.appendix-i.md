# Appendix I: Detailed Scoring Rationales and Consolidated Weighted Scoring Matrix

In Chapter 9, I conduct a comparative trade study of candidate theories, frameworks, and models of emotion. Appendix F establishes the candidate pool. Appendix G establishes the methodology for comparing those candidates, and Appendix H derives the macro- and micro-level weighting structure from that methodology. In this appendix, I apply that methodology by assigning numerical scores to each qualifying candidate across the four governing domains: definitional adequacy, analytic coherence, cognitive-semiotic adequacy, and computational adequacy.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-1">1</a></sup>

My goal in this appendix is to make the trade study fully transparent. I explain the rationale for every score assigned to each qualifying candidate and the rationale for eliminating every non-qualifying candidate. In doing so, I show why no existing theory, framework, or model provides a complete architectural foundation for constructing emotional meaning through goal-directed interpretive agency. Each qualifying candidate preserves an indispensable architectural contribution, yet none preserves the complete architecture that the Constructivist Semiotic Model of Emotions specifies.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-2">2</a></sup>

Before assigning numerical scores, I apply the dependency-ordered methodology I established in Appendices G and H. The trade study first enforces a single showstopper criterion: the definitional gate. A candidate must fix the identity conditions of emotion before I can evaluate its analytic coherence, cognitive-semiotic adequacy, or computational adequacy. Because each remaining domain presupposes a determinate object of evaluation, failure at the definitional gate immediately disqualifies a candidate from the weighted scoring matrix.

The following section lists the non-qualifying candidate theories, frameworks, and models together with the rationale explaining why each one fails the definitional gate, the trade study's sole showstopper criterion.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-3">3</a></sup>

1. Broader Psychological Constructionism

Psychological constructionism comes closest to qualification because it recognizes variability, contextual construction, and the rejection of natural-kind realism. It fails the definitional gate, however, because it does not specify which architectural constituent fixes the identity of emotion or distinguish clearly among affect, feeling, conceptualization, categorization, and emotion itself. The resulting architecture leaves emotional identity indeterminate.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-4">4</a></sup>

2. Predictive Processing / Active Inference

Predictive processing and active inference explain perception, cognition, and affect through prediction and error correction. They fail the definitional gate because they identify computational prediction rather than emotional identity as their primary explanatory object. Prediction may constrain interpretation, but it does not define what an emotion is.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-5">5</a></sup>

3. Emotion-as-Regulation Models

Emotion-as-regulation models explain how organisms manage, redirect, or regulate affective states. They fail the definitional gate because regulation presupposes an emotion to regulate. The theory therefore explains what emotions do rather than what emotions are.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-6">6</a></sup>

4. Functionalist / Evolutionary Emotion Theories

Functionalist and evolutionary theories explain emotions according to the adaptive problems they solve. They fail the definitional gate because biological function cannot by itself establish the identity conditions of emotion. Adaptive utility explains evolutionary purpose rather than emotional meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-7">7</a></sup>

5. Dimensional Models

Dimensional models organize affective states along dimensions such as valence and arousal. They fail the definitional gate because coordinates within an affective space do not distinguish emotion from affect or specify the identity conditions that differentiate fear, anger, shame, or grief.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-8">8</a></sup>

6. Basic Emotion Theory

Basic Emotion Theory identifies emotions with biologically discrete categories supported by characteristic expressions and physiological signatures. It fails the definitional gate because biological classification alone cannot distinguish affective substrate, phenomenological feeling, and cognitive-semiotic emotion as non-substitutable ontological constructs.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-9">9</a></sup>

7. Behaviorist and Associative Accounts

Behaviorist and associative accounts explain emotion through conditioning, reinforcement, and stimulus-response regularities. They fail the definitional gate immediately because they eliminate interpretation altogether, reducing emotion to behavior rather than defining it as a distinct conceptual object.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-10">10</a></sup>

The remaining four candidates satisfy the definitional gate and therefore advance to the weighted phase of the comparative trade study. The scoring tables below assign a value between 0.00 and 1.00 to each governing principle within the four evaluative domains. I compute each macro-level domain score as the weighted arithmetic mean of its corresponding micro-level principle scores using the weighting structure I established in Appendix H. These macro-level scores then produce each candidate's overall weighted score and the rationale supporting it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-11">11</a></sup> <sup><a href="../references/notes/appendix-i.md#note-12">12</a></sup>
