# Chapter 11 — Do Animals and Infants Have Emotions?

*The Difference Between Feeling and Meaning*

*Chapter 10 established the threshold: emotional meaning requires more than affect, feeling, signaling, or behavioral coordination. A felt state becomes emotional meaning only when an agent organizes it under a detachable symbolic identifier, applies it under conceptual identity conditions, and can endorse, correct, or revise that attribution. In this chapter, I bring that architecture to one of the most charged questions in the book: do animals and infants have emotions?*

*I do not ask whether animals feel. They do. I do not ask whether infants display distress, joy, attachment, or regulation. They do. I ask a narrower and more demanding question: do they cross the symbolic threshold where feeling becomes something an agent can name, stabilize, endorse, and revise? The answer depends on architecture, not sentiment. Animals show rich affective life, flexible learning, social sensitivity, and sophisticated regulation. Yet current evidence does not show, species-typically, that animals stabilize inner affective states under detachable symbolic identifiers governed by identity conditions. They can feel, signal, classify, and coordinate. They do not yet meet the strict semiotic threshold for emotional meaning.*

*Infants occupy a different position. They begin with affect and feeling before they possess emotional meaning. They cry, regulate, attach, and experience the world bodily before they can organize feeling under symbols. But unlike animals, neurotypical infants ascend developmentally into the symbolic architecture. As emotion words and reusable identifiers enter the system, children begin to stabilize felt states across contexts. Later, metarepresentational capacities allow them to revise their own attributions: what first seemed like fear may become excitement; what first seemed like anger may become embarrassment. Emotional agency emerges by development, not by mere biological activation.*

*The chapter’s central achievement is the Emotional Meaning Architecture as a threshold model. Levels 1 through 4 account for physiological activation, affective modulation, feeling, and structured proto-meaning. Level 5 marks the decisive crossing into detachable symbolic stabilization. Level 7 completes the architecture through metarepresentational endorsement, correction, and revision. This model lets me honor animal feeling without collapsing it into human emotional meaning, and it lets me explain infant development without pretending that newborn affect already carries mature emotion.*

*The result is deliberately unsentimental but not dismissive. Animals and infants can feel. Both can suffer, respond, bond, and regulate. But emotional meaning, in the strict constructivist semiotic sense, requires a further act: the organization of feeling under symbolic constraint. Animals do not yet appear to reach that threshold species-typically. Infants reach it by developmental ascent. This distinction prepares Chapter 12, where I turn from the symbolic threshold of emotion to the deeper ontological error behind our confusion: we misattribute affect, treating physiology, feeling, expression, and behavior as if they already contained emotion itself.*

Chapter 10 established the test. Emotional meaning requires a felt state to be organized under a detachable symbolic identifier governed by conceptual identity conditions, endorsed under stake, grammatically structured, and, at higher levels, subject to metarepresentational evaluation. If any of these structural constituents are absent, the system does not exhibit emotional meaning in the strict semiotic sense.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-1">1</a></sup>

I apply that architecture in this chapter to the motivating question: Do animals and infants meet these conditions?

The issue is not whether animals feel. They do. Nor do I ask whether infants display affect, distress, joy, or attachment. They do. I pose a narrower and more exacting question. Do they use symbols, endorsement, and concepts to transform affective states into detachable emotional meaning? Do they stabilize felt states under reusable identifiers that they can invoke across contexts? Can they evaluate their own emotional attributions under correctness conditions?<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-2">2</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-3">3</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-4">4</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-5">5</a></sup>

To answer this, I move from definition to discrimination. I use the Emotional Meaning Architecture as a threshold model. Each level specifies what must exist structurally. I do not interpret behavior generously or skeptically. I determine whether the necessary constituents are present.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-6">6</a></sup>

After I specified the architecture and its threshold clearly in Chapter 10, I can now ask the decisive question: Do animals and infants possess these structural conditions?

The strongest resistance to this conclusion does not come from Levels 1 through 4. It comes from contemporary animal cognition research, which appears at first glance to press directly against Level 5. Vervet monkeys produce distinct alarm calls for different predators. Scrub-jays display episodic-like memory and cache food in ways that anticipate future need. Researchers trained great apes such as Kanzi to use lexigrams in flexible ways. Dogs learn object labels across contexts. Corvids engage in strategic social deception and appear sensitive to what others know from prior visual access. These findings are impressive and must be taken seriously. The question is not whether animals categorize, generalize, plan, or signal. The question is whether any of these behaviors demonstrate detachable symbolic stabilization under conceptual identity conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-10">10</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-11">11</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-12">12</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-13">13</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-14">14</a></sup>

Distinct alarm calls demonstrate structured classification and coordinated response. They show that the animal discriminates different stimuli and maps them onto different behavioral patterns. This is already significant, but it establishes stimulus-sensitive classification rather than detachable symbolic meaning. A call may reliably track a predator type and coordinate group behavior without functioning as a reusable identifier that the animal can invoke apart from the triggering situation. What such calls do not yet demonstrate is symbolic detachment. A detachable identifier must be invocable independently of its immediate trigger and must govern the stabilization of an internal construct under identity conditions across contexts. These alarm calls function primarily as indexical signs tied to environmental triggers. There is no evidence that the animal invokes the identifier in the absence of the stimulus, endorses it as such, or treats it as something that can be correct or mistaken. The system coordinates behavior, but it does not stabilize a felt state under a reusable symbolic type. If the identifier is not detachable and type-stable across contexts, there is nothing for endorsement to endorse and nothing for revision to revise.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-15">15</a></sup>

Scrub-jay planning demonstrates flexible representation and sensitivity to future possibility. Yet episodic-like memory and caching that anticipates later need do not by themselves entail symbolic detachment. Using David Marr’s levels of analysis, these behaviors succeed at the computational level: the animal solves a problem about later access by storing food where it can retrieve it. At the algorithmic level, however, the evidence does not require detachable symbolic identifiers. The behavior can arise from memory for what, where, and when, combined with regularities the animal has acquired about decay, hunger, and cache recovery. To cross Level 5, the system must organize an affective state under a reusable identifier that conceptual identity conditions govern. To cross Level 7, it must represent that representation as representation and revise it under correctness conditions. Behavioral flexibility alone does not establish either threshold.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-16">16</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-17">17</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-18">18</a></sup>

Ape lexigram use is the strongest candidate for symbolic architecture. However, current evidence comes largely from intensive human-mediated training rather than from species-typical spontaneous use. Even in trained apes, the evidence primarily supports instrumental symbol use that operates within reward structures. It does not yet establish that apes species-typically stabilize inner affective states under detachable identifiers that identity conditions govern or that they revise such attributions reflectively. Training studies therefore provide important evidence of symbolic learning under scaffolded conditions, but they do not by themselves establish species-typical architectural attainment.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-19">19</a></sup>

Cross-context word learning in dogs shows robust category generalization, but it does not establish the threshold at issue here. Object-label learning shows that a dog can connect a sound pattern with an object type across contexts. It does not show that the dog stabilizes an inner affective state under a detachable emotional identifier such as fear, shame, or anger. The relevant question is not whether a dog can learn that “ball” refers to a class of objects, but whether it can organize its own felt state under an emotion concept that identity conditions govern and that the animal can endorse, withdraw, or revise. Current evidence supports impressive associative and categorical learning, but it does not establish symbolic stabilization of affective states.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-20">20</a></sup>

Strategic social deception in corvids suggests sensitivity to the perceptual access of others. Yet tracking what another has seen is not equivalent to representing one’s own representation as representation. Metarepresentational endorsement requires that a system treat its own attribution as something it can evaluate for correctness. Evidence of behavioral sensitivity does not by itself demonstrate reflective evaluation of internal emotional constructs.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-21">21</a></sup>

The boundary I draw is therefore architectural rather than sentimental. Signal systems may differentiate stimuli in highly specific ways. Category learning may flex across contexts. Planning may anticipate later need. Social cognition may be sophisticated. None of these, taken alone, entails that a system uses detachable symbolic identifiers, applies identity conditions, and endorses attributions under correctness conditions. The criteria were derived prior to their application to animals. If detachable stabilization and metarepresentational endorsement are necessary for intensional meaning in any domain, then their absence entails the absence of emotional meaning in the strict semiotic sense, regardless of species.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-22">22</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-23">23</a></sup>

At this point, the dispute turns on a sharper question. What, in principle, would count as evidence that an animal has crossed the symbolic threshold? If I merely define the criteria into place, then I secure the conclusion by stipulation. The threshold must therefore be behaviorally and architecturally meaningful. It must allow a system to meet it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-24">24</a></sup>

Detachable symbolic stabilization requires more than stimulus discrimination, conditioned signaling, or context-sensitive generalization. The evidence would need to show that the organism organizes an affective state under a reusable internal identifier, invokes that identifier without the original trigger, applies it across distinct contexts under stable identity conditions, and corrects misapplication as misapplication. In other words, the system would need to show not merely flexible categorization, but stabilization under conceptual norms. Here “conceptual norms” means that the identifier does not merely track similarity or reinforcement history; it fixes what counts as an admissible instance and what counts as error. This follows the inferential tradition associated with Robert Brandom and John McDowell, where meaning depends on commitments, entitlement, and correction rather than bare causal association.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-25">25</a></sup>

Metarepresentational endorsement requires more than sensitivity to another’s informational state. The organism would need to take its own emotional attribution as an object of evaluation and treat it as something it can affirm, reject, correct, revise, or negate. This need not require full natural language, but it does require symbolic or proto-syntactic structure sufficient to mark an attribution as an attribution. Without that structure, the behavior may show updating, inhibition, or social sensitivity, but it does not yet show reflective reclassification under correctness conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-26">26</a></sup>

The current animal cognition literature provides impressive demonstrations of discrimination, generalization, planning, and social sensitivity. Current animal cognition literature does not yet show, species-typically, that animals stabilize inner affective states under detachable identifiers according to conceptual norms, or that they reflectively revise such attributions under correctness conditions. On present evidence, animals therefore do not cross the threshold, not by stipulation, but because the architectural criteria require capacities that empirical research could in principle satisfy.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-27">27</a></sup>

A final clarification is necessary. It may be granted that certain animals use arbitrary symbols, exhibit referential displacement, and participate in shared conventional signaling. None of these features, taken alone or together, yet establishes intensional identity under correctness conditions. A lexigram such as “hurt” may be applied to bodily distress. A child may use a gesture to request cessation. A vocalization may refer to an absent predator. But detachable symbolic stabilization, in the sense I define here, requires more than arbitrariness, displacement, or shared interpretability. It requires that the identifier function as a governing constraint fixing what counts as a legitimate instance across contexts, such that misapplication is possible and correction is intelligible.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-28">28</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-29">29</a></sup>

The decisive question is not whether an animal can produce a symbol in the absence of a stimulus, nor whether others can interpret it. The question is whether the organism treats the symbol as normatively binding on its own classification of inner states. To demonstrate Level 5, there would need to be evidence that the identifier regulates admissibility under stable identity conditions rather than tracking reinforcement history or instrumental success. To demonstrate Level 7, there would need to be evidence that the organism can take its own emotional attribution as an object of evaluation and revise it under reasons. Present evidence of symbol use that animals acquire through training, however sophisticated, does not yet establish this architectural form.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-30">30</a></sup>

The criteria therefore do not exclude the empirical cases by definition. They distinguish between symbol use as behavioral coordination and symbol-governed stabilization of affect under intensional constraint. The former appears impressive and widespread. Current evidence has not established the latter species-typically.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-31">31</a></sup>

Consider a student who receives critical feedback in front of peers. At Level 4, the student experiences arousal and discomfort toward the evaluative event. The state stands for the situation. It organizes orientation and behavior relative to that social target. The student feels exposure or unease. This structured affect represents and targets the evaluative event, but it remains pre-symbolic. The student undergoes the feeling but has not yet stabilized it under a detachable identifier. This constitutes structured affective proto-meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-32">32</a></sup>

At Level 5, the architecture changes. The student organizes the felt state under a reusable symbolic identifier such as “embarrassment.” Conceptual identity conditions govern the identifier, and the student can invoke it independently of the immediate event. The student can later recall the episode and say, “I was embarrassed,” even in the absence of the original context. The student no longer merely feels the state. The student takes it up as something. By stabilizing it under a detachable symbolic identifier, the student brings proto-emotional meaning into existence. The student has crossed the symbolic threshold.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-33">33</a></sup>

By contrast, when a dominance display confronts a dog, the dog may exhibit arousal, avoidance, and structured threat classification. At Level 4, the state stands for a social target and organizes behavior relative to hierarchy. The response may be complex and context-sensitive. Yet current evidence does not show that the dog stabilizes the felt state under a detachable symbolic identifier that it can invoke across contexts or endorse as such. The response remains dependent on the stimulus. It does not cross into Level 5. The difference is architectural. The adolescent can organize the state under a reusable identifier that conceptual constraint governs. The animal exhibits structured affect without detaching it symbolically.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-34">34</a></sup>

I must clarify the point here, because critics may object that animals clearly categorize. They discriminate predators from non-predators. They generalize across contexts. They track social rank and prior interactions. If such cross-context generalization exists, why is this not already conceptual identity under detachable symbolic constraint?

The answer lies in the difference between category formation and identity conditions that symbols govern.

Non-symbolic category learning operates through statistical regularities, feature clustering, and behavioral generalization. A system may reliably group stimuli that share relevant features and adjust behavior accordingly. Animals may group stimuli flexibly, context-sensitively, and with remarkable sophistication. It may support transfer across situations and even anticipatory planning. Yet this form of organization still depends on stimuli. The category functions as a regulatory pattern within the system. It does not exist as a detachable identifier that can be invoked independently of its triggering conditions.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-35">35</a></sup>

Symbolic identity conditions require more. They require a symbolic type that stabilizes admissibility conditions for its instantiations. The identifier must be reusable across contexts, callable in the absence of the immediate stimulus, and subject to endorsement as such. It must fix what counts as a legitimate instance under intensional constraint, rather than merely reflecting similarity-based clustering or reinforcement history.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-36">36</a></sup>

A predator category in an animal may guide avoidance across contexts. But unless the animal stabilizes that category under a detachable symbolic identifier, invokes it, endorses it, and applies it independently of immediate cues, the category remains a regulatory construct rather than a semiotic one. The system responds with sensitivity to regularities; it does not take the state as something under a reusable symbolic type.

The distinction therefore does not rest on behavioral complexity. It rests on architectural form. Non-symbolic categorization organizes behavior through regularities the animal has come to track. Symbolic identity conditions organize constructs under detachable, endorsable identifiers that fix admissibility and permit reflective evaluation. Level 4 permits the former. Level 5 requires the latter.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-37">37</a></sup>

Until the system meets that latter condition, it remains within affective proto-meaning rather than proto-emotional meaning in the strict semiotic sense.

At Level 7, a further transformation occurs. The adolescent can represent the emotional representation itself. The student may reflect, “Perhaps this is not humiliation but constructive criticism,” or, “I misread the situation.” The original emotional construct becomes an object of evaluation. The adolescent can revise, justify, or withdraw it. Correctness conditions now operate. Emotional meaning in the full sense requires this metarepresentational endorsement.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-38">38</a></sup>

A serious challenge to the threshold model arises from research suggesting that some nonhuman animals track the knowledge states of others. Scrub-jays re-cache food when another bird has previously observed them, as if aware that another bird may remember the cache location. Certain apes alter behavior depending on what a competitor has seen. Dogs sometimes display behaviors that owners and researchers describe as guilt-like when owners confront them. Researchers frequently cite these findings as evidence that animals possess metarepresentational capacities. The decisive question, however, is not whether animals behave as if they are sensitive to informational states. It is what kind of representation is operative in those cases.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-39">39</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-40">40</a></sup>,<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-41">41</a></sup>

Metarepresentation in the strict semiotic sense does not consist in flexible behavior or even in tracking the perceptual access of another. It consists in representing a representation as representation. It requires that a system take its own attribution as an object of evaluation, capable of endorsement, withdrawal, revision, or negation under correctness conditions. The architecture must allow the agent not merely to respond under a representation, but to stand in reflective relation to it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-42">42</a></sup>

A scrub-jay that re-caches food after another bird observes it may track the other bird’s perceptual access. That capacity may take the form of second-order representation: other-bird-saw-cache. But this remains a representation about another’s perceptual state. It does not entail that the jay represents its own representation as a representation. There is no evidence that the bird can evaluate the correctness of its attribution, revise it under reasons, or negate it as mistaken. The behavior may show sophistication and context sensitivity while it remains entirely first-order in structure. This deflationary reading should acknowledge a complication from Clayton's own later work, Emery and Clayton (2001) found that scrub jays who had themselves pilfered another bird's cache were more likely to re-cache food when watched, suggesting the behavior may draw on the bird's own thieving experience rather than purely first-order perceptual tracking. This does not by itself establish metarepresentation, but it is a stronger finding than simple gaze-tracking and deserves acknowledgment before the first-order conclusion is drawn. <sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-43">43</a></sup>

Similarly, when apes adjust actions depending on what a competitor has seen, the operative architecture may encode conditional mappings between observed gaze and subsequent opportunity. Even if this involves representing another’s informational state, it does not follow that the ape can take its own attribution as an object of reflective assessment. Tracking another’s knowledge is not equivalent to reflecting upon one’s own representational act.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-44">44</a></sup>

The same distinction applies to canine “guilt-like” behavior. Dogs exhibit submissive postures when confronted by owners after rule violations. Yet experimental work suggests that such displays correlate more reliably with the owner’s scolding behavior than with the dog’s own transgression history. Even if the dog associates past action with anticipated response, this does not demonstrate that it represents its prior emotional state as an emotional attribution subject to correction. Behavioral contingency and metarepresentational endorsement are not the same.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-45">45</a></sup>

I therefore do not defend linguistic introspection as the threshold here. It does not require verbal confession or explicit self-narration. It requires structural capacity for reflective stance toward one’s own emotional construct. A system that can say, implicitly or explicitly, “I was wrong to classify this as fear,” exhibits metarepresentation. A system that merely adjusts behavior under changing contingencies does not. The difference is not one of complexity, but of architectural level.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-46">46</a></sup>

Current evidence does not show that animals reach Level 7. Without detachable symbolic stabilization at Level 5 and without reflective revision at Level 7, affect may retain organization, but it does not become fully semiotic. Emotional meaning in the strict sense requires symbolic detachment and metarepresentational endorsement.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-47">47</a></sup>

<p align="center">**Table 11-2. Neurotypical Infant Development from Affective Activation to Full Emotional Meaning**</p>
Table 11-2 traces the developmental ascent toward emotional meaning. The age ranges are approximate and represent typical developmental windows rather than rigid thresholds. The architecture is the constant; developmental timing may vary across individuals.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-48">48</a></sup>

The early levels display physiological activation, affective modulation, conscious feeling, and increasingly differentiated affective organization. These stages demonstrate that infants experience and regulate affect long before symbolic stabilization emerges. Feeling precedes the symbol.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-49">49</a></sup>

The decisive transition does not occur merely when a child acquires emotion vocabulary. Lexical labeling alone does not constitute detachable symbolic stabilization. A child may repeat an emotion word without yet organizing felt states under stable identity conditions across contexts. What matters is not word production, but whether the child uses the identifier as a reusable, cross-context stabilizer that conceptual constraint governs.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-50">50</a></sup>

Level 5 therefore requires more than the utterance of words such as “sad” or “mad.” It requires that the child organize affect under a detachable identifier that the child can invoke in the absence of the immediate stimulus, apply across varied contexts, and endorse as operative. The identifier must function under implicit identity conditions that distinguish it from neighboring states. Only then does proto-emotional meaning emerge.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-51">51</a></sup>

In typical development, this stabilization appears during the early preschool years, though timing varies and the boundary is functional rather than lexical. What marks the transition is not vocabulary size but cross-situational application, self-attribution, and consistent differentiation among emotional categories.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-52">52</a></sup>

Full emotional meaning requires a further transformation. At Level 7, the child can represent the emotional representation itself. The child may revise an earlier attribution, recognize misclassification, or justify an emotional judgment relative to reasons. Here metarepresentation enters, and correctness conditions become explicit.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-53">53</a></sup>

The threshold for emotional meaning in infancy therefore lies not at speech acquisition, but at the emergence of detachable symbolic stabilization under identity conditions. Full maturity requires reflective endorsement and revision.

The sequence follows from the structural dependencies I identified in Chapter 10. Feeling precedes symbolic stabilization, and symbolic stabilization precedes reflective correction. Without these architectural developments, emotional meaning does not arise within the subject.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-54">54</a></sup>

In this chapter I treated the question of emotion as a question of meaning. Feeling, expression, and regulation can occur without emotional meaning because they can occur without semiotic architecture.

This is the decisive threshold. On the Emotional Meaning Table, it corresponds to Level 5: detachable symbolic stabilization. Full emotional meaning requires more than stabilization. It requires metarepresentational endorsement: the capacity to evaluate, revise, negate, or justify one’s own emotional attribution under correctness conditions. On the table, that corresponds to Level 7.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-55">55</a></sup>

With this criterion in place, the comparative result is straightforward. Non-human animals reliably reach Levels 1 through 4. They exhibit physiological activation, affective modulation, feeling, and structured representational organization of behavior relative to social targets and objects. They can regulate, learn, generalize, and coordinate signals. What the current evidence does not establish species-typically is Level 5: the stabilization of inner states under detachable, endorsable identifiers. Without Level 5, there is no proto-emotional meaning in the strict semiotic sense. Because current evidence does not establish Level 5, it also does not establish Level 7 species-typically. Animals therefore qualify for affect, feeling, and sophisticated regulation, but they do not meet the threshold for emotional meaning.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-56">56</a></sup>

Infants, by contrast, cross the threshold developmentally. They begin at Levels 0 through 4, with arousal, affective modulation, conscious feeling, and early non-symbolic organization of affect. They do not possess emotional meaning at birth. Proto-emotional meaning emerges when detachable identifiers enter the system and the child can apply them to felt states across contexts.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-57">57</a></sup>

On the table, that occurs at Level 5, typically around 2 to 3 years, when emotion words and other detachable identifiers begin to stabilize self-attributed constructs. Full emotional meaning emerges later when metarepresentational capacities develop and the child can reflectively revise an attribution, such as recognizing that an earlier “fear” was actually excitement. On the table, that corresponds to Level 7, typically around 4 to 5 years.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-58">58</a></sup>

Animals and infants can feel. Both can display complex behavior. Emotional meaning, however, requires a specific semiotic achievement. On current evidence, nonhuman animals do not yet appear to reach that threshold species-typically, but I do not treat that conclusion as settled by definition. Cognitive science continues to debate the scope of animal symbolic cognition, shared intentionality, mindreading, and representational flexibility. Michael Tomasello emphasizes the distinctive role of shared intentionality in human cognition, while Daniel Dennett argues that intentional interpretation can reveal complex forms of animal agency without requiring human-style linguistic self-report. Humans do reach the symbolic threshold, and infants do so by developmental ascent, first crossing Level 5 and later achieving full emotional meaning at Level 7.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-59">59</a></sup>

If emotional meaning requires detachable symbolic stabilization and metarepresentational endorsement, then emotion cannot be identical to physiology, arousal, expression, or even phenomenological feeling. Those phenomena can occur in the absence of symbolic architecture. Emotional meaning therefore cannot be located at the level of affective circuitry or bodily change alone.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-60">60</a></sup>

Yet it would be equally mistaken to deny the reality of affect. Organisms undeniably undergo shifts in arousal, tension, attraction, aversion, depletion, and activation. These changes are measurable, reportable, and experientially present. They precede symbolic stabilization. They can occur in animals and in pre-symbolic infants. What they lack is not reality, but semiotic organization.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-61">61</a></sup>

The distinction I establish in this chapter therefore yields a new explanatory task. If animals possess affect and feeling without emotional meaning, and if infants begin life with affective experience that becomes consciously available before symbolic stabilization, then I should not treat bodily affect, phenomenological feeling, and emotional meaning as identical. They may emerge in close coordination, and phenomenologists may rightly emphasize their lived unity. My architectural claim is narrower: emotional meaning requires a further semiotic act. It arises when an agent organizes feeling under a detachable symbolic identifier, conceptual constraint, context, and stake. The subject feels that change. The agent interprets it.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-62">62</a></sup>

I have resolved the question of emotional meaning architecturally. In this model, semiotics governs the overall architecture, Kripkean identity conditions govern conceptual admissibility, and Brandomian normativity governs endorsement, correction, and revision. Animals exhibit affect, feeling, and sophisticated regulation, but current evidence does not show that they stabilize inner affective states under detachable symbolic identifiers or revise such attributions under correctness conditions. Infants begin with affect and feeling, then cross the symbolic threshold developmentally. Emotional meaning is therefore a constructed semiotic achievement, not a primitive biological given.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-63">63</a></sup>

This conclusion forces a deeper inquiry. If emotional meaning depends upon symbolic organization, then emotion cannot be identical to physiology, arousal, expression, or phenomenological feeling. Those phenomena can occur without detachable symbolic architecture. Yet affect is undeniably real. Organisms undergo measurable and lived shifts in arousal, tension, attraction, aversion, depletion, and activation. These changes precede interpretation. <sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-64">64</a></sup>

The distinction now becomes ontological. If biology grounds affect and symbolic construction produces emotion, then an agent must organize emotion from affect rather than treat emotion as identical with affect.<sup><a href="../references/notes/chapter-11.md#note-65">65</a></sup>

The next chapter examines that distinction directly. It asks whether emotions are objective psychological states or constructed configurations that arise when affect is organized under symbolic constraint.
